### PAGE 4 — The boast.
**PANEL 1.** Flashback-toned panel, the day before: the hunt in the margins of the sacred grove — [AGAMEMNON] loosing an arrow; the white-flanked HIND of the grove leaping, struck, falling among the oaks.
> CAPTION: For the day before, hunting the margins of the grove, the king of kings had loosed at a hind — the hind — and struck her clean at long range. It was, in truth, a magnificent shot.
**PANEL 2.** [AGAMEMNON] over the fallen deer among his huntsmen, flushed with triumph, arms spread — the boast leaving his mouth.
> AGAMEMNON: HA! Artemis herself could not have shot better!
**PANEL 3.** The grove around them: every leaf suddenly still; the huntsmen's smiles dying one by one; a cold that is not weather.
> CAPTION: Eight words, princes. Count them. He killed the goddess's own creature on the goddess's own ground, and then measured his hand against hers aloud. In the first song a king cheated gods of a wage. This king did worse: he told a god she was second. The grove said nothing at all. The grove did not need to.

### PAGE 5 — The dead sea.
**PANEL 1 (wide).** [SET-AULIS], weeks on: the bay glass-flat under white haze, a thousand sails slack, heat-shimmer; men rowing water-casks, listless.
> CAPTION: From that day, no wind. Not storm — nothing. A sea like poured metal, week upon week, while a hundred thousand men drank the springs low and ate the country bare.
**PANEL 2.** The fraying camp: a fight over a water-skin broken up; sick men in hut-shade; kings arguing in knots; [ODYSSEUS] watching the slack sails, then the grove, and beginning to understand.
> CAPTION: An army is a city with no farmers. It eats itself. Sickness came, and quarrels, and the first talk of giving up the war entire. And a few men — the kind who watch — began to look from the dead sails to the silent grove, and back.
**PANEL 3.** [CALCHAS] standing apart at the tide-line, pale eyes on the grove, his face a locked door.
> CAPTION: Calchas the seer had known for a month. He had not spoken, because some knowledge is a death-sentence to its speaker. At last the kings compelled him under oath of protection. And he spoke.

### PAGE 6 — The price.
**PANEL 1.** The council tent, torchlit: [CALCHAS] speaking with his eyes shut; the ring of kings; [AGAMEMNON] whitening.
> CALCHAS: The goddess of the wild holds the wind, and her price is set. Not hecatombs. Not gold. The king who matched his hand against hers pays with what his boast was worth to him: the life of his eldest daughter. Iphigenia — or no wind, ever.
**PANEL 2.** [AGAMEMNON] on his feet, the scepter clattering down.
> AGAMEMNON: NO. Disband the army! Burn the ships! I unsay the oath — I unsay the war —!
**PANEL 3.** And around him: silence, and the eyes of the kings — [MENELAUS]'s need, [ODYSSEUS]'s watchfulness, the tent full of the oath he built; his own words closing on him like the trap they always were.
> CAPTION: And he meant it — for the length of one evening he meant it. But he had built a machine of oaths and kings and gathered Greece twice, and a man who has made himself the head of such a machine learns its first law: it does not disband. By morning the messengers were riding to Mycenae — with a lie.
> FOOTNOTE: *The lie and its apparatus are told most fully by the later tragedians; the old song of the Cycle knows the summons and the altar. I give the lie one page, because without it you cannot weigh the queen.

### PAGE 7 — The wedding that was not. *(Tier C, flagged)*
**PANEL 1.** The Mycenae road: a bright traveling party — [CLYTEMNESTRA] radiant with pride, [IPHIGENIA] beside her in wedding saffron, garlands on the mule-cart. (births refs/iphigenia.png)
> CAPTION: The letter said: send Iphigenia to Aulis, to be married before the sailing — to Achilles himself, the best of the Achaeans. And so they came in wedding clothes: the queen glowing with the match, and the girl — the girl came bravely and gladly, princes, which is the part I would spare you if sparing were my trade.
**PANEL 2.** The camp: [ACHILLES] learning his name was used — the beautiful face going white, then terrible; [PATROCLUS]'s hand hard on his arm.
> ACHILLES: They wrote MY name on it? My name — to bait a child to an altar? Patroclus — my NAME —
> CAPTION: Achilles had known nothing. When he learned what his name had been made to carry, he was for wrecking the whole design, sword in hand, army or no army. Remember his rage here, princes: the first time you see it, it is entirely righteous. That is what makes his rages so dangerous — they always begin by being right.
**PANEL 3.** [IPHIGENIA] before her father: the wedding pretense dead; the truth told; and the girl — after the storm of weeping — going still, and straightening, and looking at the becalmed fleet through the tent door.
> CAPTION: The truth could not be held from her in that camp of whispers. She wept — she was fourteen. And then she stopped weeping, and looked a long time at the thousand slack sails, and at her father's ruined face. What she decided then, she decided herself. Hold to that. It is the one freedom no one could take from her, and she took it up like a queen.

### PAGE 8 — SPLASH: THE ALTAR AND THE HIND.
**PANEL 1 (full page).** The altar of Artemis at the grove's edge at first light, the whole army ranked in terrible silence down the shore: [IPHIGENIA] standing at the altar unbound, chin high, in the saffron dress — her own walk, her own choice visible in her bearing; [CALCHAS] with the knife lifted and his face averted; [AGAMEMNON] with his cloak pulled over his head in the ancient gesture of a grief past looking at; and breaking over the scene like a wave of silver light, half-manifest, [ARTEMIS] descending with her arms out — and beneath the falling knife, materializing in mist on the altar stone, a white HIND; the girl's form already lifting away into the goddess's radiance, transparent, ascending. (births refs/artemis.png)
> CAPTION (ornate, top): So Iphigenia came to the altar in her wedding dress, and came unbound — for she had forbidden them to bind her, saying a king's daughter needed no rope to keep a bargain. And the knife fell.
> CAPTION (ornate, bottom): And the knife found — a deer. The old song is plain, princes, and I sing it plain: at the last instant Artemis took the girl and left a hind in the mist, and bore Iphigenia away over the sea's edge to serve her, deathless, in a far country. The army saw mist and blood and believed what armies believe. Agamemnon, his head covered, saw nothing at all. And so he sailed for ten years of war believing he had killed his daughter — and his queen believed it too. Heaven spared the girl, princes. It spared no one else in that family anything. Whether that is mercy, you may argue with your own gods.

### PAGE 9 — The wind.
**PANEL 1.** The mist still on the altar — and the sails down the whole bay filling at once, banners cracking taut; the army's roar beginning.
> CAPTION: The wind came before the altar-fire was cold. A clean, strong, perfect sailing wind — the god's receipt, princes, stamped on the whole sky.
**PANEL 2.** The embarkation in wild joy; and through it, against it: [CLYTEMNESTRA] standing alone on the emptying shore, the wedding garlands from the cart scattered at her feet, watching the fleet go.
> CAPTION: They sailed cheering. Behind them on the shore stood the queen of Mycenae, holding a bride's garland, alone with what she believed — and what she believed was: my husband lured our child with a wedding and cut her throat for a wind. Enter it in the ledger, princes, in letters as deep as the ledger cuts: from this shore, Clytemnestra's road runs straight for ten years to a bath and an axe. When we come there, remember Aulis. The songs will demand you hold both in your hands at once.
**PANEL 3.** [AGAMEMNON] at the flagship's stern, not looking back; the shore, and the small figure on it, dwindling.
> CAPTION: The king of kings did not look back. He was learning the commander's art: that a man can sail away from anything, provided he never turns his head. It is not a true art. The things follow.

### PAGE 10 — Tenedos.
**PANEL 1.** The island of Tenedos off the Trojan coast: the fleet at anchor, a victory feast of the first landfall on the beach; [PHILOCTETES] among the captains, the great horn bow of Heracles beside him. (births refs/philoctetes.png)
> CAPTION: One landfall short of Troy, at the island of Tenedos, they feasted. Among the captains sat Philoctetes of Malis — bearing, as I told you in the wedding-court, the bow of Heracles itself, left to him at the hero's death.
**PANEL 2.** THE BITE: at the sacred spring's stones, a WATER-SNAKE striking [PHILOCTETES] at the foot; his cry; the feast overturning.
> CAPTION: And at the spring a water-snake struck his foot. A small wound. The gods' work often starts small.
**PANEL 3.** Days on, aboard: the wound swollen black, unhealing; [PHILOCTETES]'s ceaseless cries ringing across the anchorage; men covering their ears at the oars; the rites disrupted; kings conferring darkly.
> CAPTION: The wound would not heal and would not kill. It stank past bearing, and his crying never stopped — through every sacrifice, every council, every night. An army can carry its dead, princes. It has never learned to carry its screaming.

### PAGE 11 — Lemnos.
**PANEL 1.** The council: [ODYSSEUS] speaking the cold necessity; [AGAMEMNON] nodding; others looking away.
> ODYSSEUS: The rites are fouled and the men unmanned by it. Set him ashore on Lemnos — food, his bow, shelter of the caves. When he heals, we fetch him. It is that, or the army breaks before Troy is even sighted.
> CAPTION: It was Odysseus's counsel, and it was — hear the whole truth — probably right, and entirely merciless. Most of his counsels were both. Learn to hold that pair together too.
**PANEL 2.** The abandonment: [PHILOCTETES] asleep from exhaustion under a cave-brow on a wild shore; supplies stacked; the boats pulling away quietly; his bow beside him.
> CAPTION: They did it while he slept, like thieves of a man's whole world. He woke alone on Lemnos with his wound, his bow, and ten years of silence beginning.
**PANEL 3.** The marooned archer small on the empty shore, the fleet's sails going; his fist raised; the great bow black against the sky.
> CAPTION: Ledger, princes: on that beach sits the bow of Heracles, and in that man burns a grievance with ten years to compound. The day will come when Troy cannot fall without that bow — and the army that marooned him must come back to this beach and ask. I would not miss that conversation for a kingdom. Patience.

### PAGE 12 — The walls at last.
**PANEL 1 (wide).** Dawn off Troy: the whole fleet standing in toward the long beach — and beyond the plain, real at last, the sloping walls, the great bastion, the gate-towers of Troy; on the walls, tiny, the glitter of waiting spears.
> CAPTION: And then — Troy. After the oath, the false war, the scattering, the altar, the years: the walls themselves, exactly as rich and exactly as high as the songs had promised. And manned. Hector had used the years too.
**PANEL 2.** Aboard the lead ships: the army's eagerness curdling — for a PROPHECY runs the benches, whispered man to man; warriors suddenly finding reasons to check their sandal-straps.
> CAPTION: But no ship beached. For an oracle had run through the fleet like cold water: THE FIRST MAN ASHORE DIES. And a hundred thousand heroes discovered, all together, a deep interest in going second.
**PANEL 3.** On one deck: a young lord of Thessaly — PROTESILAUS, plain-armored, newly married — looking from the hesitating fleet to the beach, and setting his jaw.
> CAPTION: Protesilaus of Phylace had been married forty days when the summons came. His house was half-built; the songs remember that detail — a house half-built. He looked down the line of stalled ships, and he understood what the whole war was waiting for. Someone always has to be the door.

### PAGE 13 — The first death.
**PANEL 1.** THE LEAP: Protesilaus vaulting the bow into the surf, first of the whole army, spear high — one man running up an empty beach toward a waiting city.
> CAPTION: He jumped first, and ran, and for a dozen heartbeats the war was one man's footprints on wet sand.
**PANEL 2.** From the Trojan line at the dune-crest: [HECTOR] coming down to meet him — the crown prince himself, taking the city's first spear-work as his own duty, no other Trojan moving; the two closing.
> CAPTION: And Troy answered him with its best. Hector came down the dune alone — for the crown prince held that the city's first blood was the crown prince's work and no lesser man's. Even the Greeks, watching, understood they were seeing how this particular enemy would fight the whole war: in front.
**PANEL 3.** After: Protesilaus fallen on the sand with dignity, face to the sky; [HECTOR] standing over him a moment — then raising his spear NOT in triumph but in salute, before the roaring fleet; the landing crashing ashore behind.
> CAPTION: The oracle was paid. Protesilaus, first ashore, first dead — killed by Hector, saluted by Hector. Far away in Thessaly a bride named Laodamia would grieve so hard that the songs still flinch from her pages; the half-built house was never finished. Enter the first name of the war's true bill, princes. There will be others. There will be a nation of others. But the first is the one the songs keep tender.

### PAGE 14 — Achilles ashore.
**PANEL 1 (wide).** The full landing: the beach a storm of hulls and spears; and through the Trojan counterattack a giant of a man wading — CYCNUS, son of Poseidon, white-skinned as sea-foam, spears SHATTERING on his hide as on rock; the landing stalling around him.
> CAPTION: The Trojans hurled the landing back at the tide-line behind a champion out of the old world: Cycnus, a son of Poseidon, whom no bronze could bite. Spears broke on him like reeds. The whole beachhead began to die around one unwoundable man.
**PANEL 2.** [ACHILLES] meeting him — bronze useless — and changing the problem: the shield-rim, the helmet-strap, raw wrestling strength, bearing the giant down into the surf by main force and fury.
> CAPTION: So Achilles put down the spear that pierces everything, and solved the man the other way. What bronze cannot bite, princes, weight and will still can. He bore him down into the sea his father ruled, and held him there, and the sea took its own son back. Poseidon marked the boy who did it. Gods always mark the hands that cost them.
**PANEL 3.** The rout: Trojans streaming back across the plain for the gates; the Greeks holding the beach; [HECTOR] last through the closing gate, rearguard to his own retreat.
> CAPTION: With Cycnus gone the line broke, and Troy went back behind its walls — Hector last through the gate, as he would be last a hundred times. The Greeks held the beach. They would hold that beach, and little more than that beach, for nine years. Now you know the shape of the whole war, princes: a locked door, and a hundred thousand men camped on the doormat.

### PAGE 15 — The frame takes breath. [STYLE-FRAME]
**PANEL 1.** The hall; the NELEID-PRINCE.
> NELEID-PRINCE: The first man ashore. Father — did Protesilaus believe the oracle?
**PANEL 2.** The SINGER.

### PAGE 16 — The embassy enters Troy.
**PANEL 1 (wide).** The great gate of Troy opening to a herald's staff: [MENELAUS] and [ODYSSEUS] entering the enemy city unarmed under the sacred immunity of envoys, spear-lined streets watching; hate and awe on the crowded walls.
> CAPTION: Before the siege closed, the Greeks sent two men into Troy under the herald's staff, to demand Helen and the treasure back and end the war at a stroke: Menelaus the wronged, and Odysseus the tongue.
**PANEL 2.** The house of [ANTENOR]: the old counselor receiving the envoys at his own hearth, his household serving them — guest-law inside the enemy's wall.
> CAPTION: And in all Troy one house took them in: Antenor's. He fed them at his hearth and set his own honor around them like a wall — for envoys are sacred, and Antenor was the last man in that city keeping every law at once. Mark this hearth well. On the night Troy burns, remember who ate at it.
**PANEL 3.** Night, the guest-chamber: [ODYSSEUS] at the window looking out over the torchlit city — measuring streets, gates, walls with those grey eyes; filing it all.
> CAPTION: And Odysseus, guest and envoy, honorable to the letter — looked out all night at the streets of Troy, and measured them, and forgot nothing. He would walk them twice more before the end, princes: once in rags, and once in fire.

### PAGE 17 — The assembly refuses.
**PANEL 1.** The Trojan assembly in the gate-court: [MENELAUS] speaking his demand — plain, short, iron; [PRIAM] on the dais; [PARIS-prince] among his faction, jaw set; the crowd loud.
> CAPTION: Before the assembled city Menelaus spoke briefly, as wronged men do: my wife, my treasure, and we sail; refuse, and we stay until your city is ash. Then Odysseus rose.
**PANEL 2.** [ODYSSEUS] speaking: stock-still, staff planted, eyes down — and then the voice: the assembly leaning in against its own will; even hostile faces caught.
> CAPTION: He stood oddly, they said after — stiff, staring at the ground like a man who had never spoken in public — until the voice came, and the words fell like winter snow, thick and unanswerable, and there was no man in the court who did not lean toward them. Antenor remembered that speech all his life; the great singer has him describe it, years on, word for word. Troy heard, that day, exactly what it was refusing.
**PANEL 3.** The refusal: ANTIMACHUS — a heavy gold-hung noble — on his feet bellowing, gold visibly new on his arms, demanding the envoys be killed where they stand; uproar; [ANTENOR] and his sons physically shielding the two Greeks; [PRIAM]'s staff striking the ground for order.
> CAPTION: Then Antimachus stood — a lord whose new gold had Paris's fingerprints all over it — and roared that the envoys should die in the court, sacred staff and all. It nearly carried. Antenor's own body and Priam's own staff held the law upright, barely. But enter Antimachus and his bought gold in the ledger, princes: a day comes, on the battlefield, when his two sons will kneel and beg an Atreid for their lives — and be answered with their father's words. The songs return everything. Everything.

### PAGE 18 — War, lawfully.
**PANEL 1.** The envoys walking back down from the gate between the spear-lines, unharmed, refused; the great gate booming shut behind them.
> CAPTION: So the demand was made in full law, and in full law refused. The envoys walked out untouched — Troy kept that much — and the gate shut, and there was nothing left between the two peoples but the plain.
**PANEL 2.** [PRIAM] alone after the assembly, old hands on the parapet, Ida beyond.
> CAPTION: Understand Priam's arithmetic one last time before the years close over it: to give her back was to hand a suppliant of his hearth to her enemies and unmake his kingship's law; to keep her was war with the world. He chose his law and his son. A wrong choice? Princes, I have sung this song a hundred times, and I can tell you only this: I have never once found the page where a right choice was available to him. Some men are billed for the crossroads, not the turning.
**PANEL 3.** The two camps at dusk: the city walls torchlit, the beach camp's first palisade stakes going up; between them the empty plain.
> CAPTION: And so it became what it became: the war of the age, entered by both sides with the forms observed — demand made, demand refused. When your priests tell you, princes, that a war can be lawful and a calamity at once: this one. This exact one.

### PAGE 19 — SPLASH: THE NINE YEARS.
**PANEL 1 (full page).** The great wheel of the years, one composition: the beach camp and the walls facing each other across the plain while around and through the scene the seasons turn in bands — palisade timbers rising and silvering; raid-fleets launching down the coast and returning low-laden; funeral pyres and funeral games; snow on the huts, poppies on the plain; boys of the camp becoming spearmen; the walls of Troy unchanged through every band, serene, closed.
> CAPTION (ornate, top): Then the years. The great singer leaps them in a line; grant me one picture. Nine years the Greeks held the beach and could not take the wall; nine years Troy held the wall and could not lift the siege.
> CAPTION (ornate, bottom): The war found its shape and its economy: the Greeks raided the whole coast for bread and plunder — twenty-three cities the songs credit to Achilles alone — and Troy bled gold to feed allies, and the plain between drank a generation. Men who had sailed as bridegrooms sent word to sons who could now lift a spear. And the stone serpent's count ran quietly down toward the tenth year, in which everything in the remaining songs occurs. These middle years I give you in three raids and one crime, princes — chosen because the greatest song of all stands on exactly those four stones.

### PAGE 20 — Lyrnessus: Briseis.
**PANEL 1.** The raid on Lyrnessus: the town taken; and amid the aftermath-smoke, brought out among the captives, [BRISEIS] — walking unbowed through the ruin of her world. (births refs/briseis.png)
> CAPTION: In the raid-years Achilles took Lyrnessus, and in it Briseis — a king's daughter-in-law; her husband and her three brothers fell in that one morning. The songs do not pretend otherwise, and neither will I: this is what the raiding was, for those raided.
**PANEL 2.** The Myrmidon camp after: [BRISEIS] before [ACHILLES] — and the surprise of the age: the terrible young man treating the grieving woman with grave, careful courtesy; [PATROCLUS] near, kind.
> CAPTION: And then the strange thing, which Briseis herself will one day say over the dead with the whole army listening: in the camp of the man who had ruined her, she found — gentleness. Achilles was to her, in her own remembered words, kind always.
**PANEL 3.** A quiet dusk panel: [PATROCLUS] speaking gently to [BRISEIS] apart, her face lifting a fraction for the first time.
> PATROCLUS: Bear it, lady. I tell you this is not your end. When the war lets him, I will see you made not his prize but his wife — this I promise you.
> CAPTION: And gentle Patroclus promised her a future — marriage, honor, a home in Phthia — and she, having nothing else, planted her heart on the promise. Ledger it in gold and tears, princes: you will hear Briseis say these very words back, over a body, in the greatest lament of the great song. I will not tell you whose body. You already dread correctly.

### PAGE 21 — Thebe: the house of Eetion.
**PANEL 1.** The raid on Thebe-under-Plakos: the city of old King EETION taken; Achilles's Myrmidons in the citadel; and [CHRYSEIS], caught visiting from her father's shrine-town, among the captives. (births refs/chryseis.png)
> CAPTION: Another season, another city: Thebe of King Eetion. Among the captives taken there was Chryseis — not even a woman of Thebe, princes; a priest's daughter visiting kin, swept up by the day's arithmetic. Hold her name. She is small in this song and enormous in the next.
**PANEL 2.** The strange honor: [ACHILLES] standing over the fallen old KING EETION — and ordering him burned in his full armor, a mound raised, nothing stripped; Myrmidons planting elm saplings around the mound.
> CAPTION: Eetion the old king died fighting, and Achilles — mark him, for the man is never one thing — took no spoil from the body, but burned him in his armor with full honor and raised his mound, and the mountain nymphs, the songs say, planted elms around it. He could kill a whole house before noon and honor it perfectly by dusk, and mean both. That is not a contradiction, princes. That is the heroic age, in one man.
**PANEL 3.** Troy, the aftermath arriving: [ANDROMACHE] — daughter of Eetion, wife of Hector — receiving the news of her father and seven brothers, all in a day; [HECTOR] coming to her fast across the room, catching her. (births refs/andromache.png)
> CAPTION: And in Troy the news came home to Eetion's daughter: father dead, seven brothers dead, mother soon after — her whole first world erased in one raiding-season, by one man. Her name is Andromache, and she is Hector's wife. Now hear what she will say to her husband, years hence, in the tenderest scene the great singer ever made, and understand where it was forged: "Hector — you are my father now, and my mother, and my brothers, and my husband." Every word of that sentence, princes, was purchased on this page. The Iliad is not long from here. It is being loaded, beam by beam, before your eyes.

### PAGE 22 — Lycaon.
**PANEL 1.** Night in Priam's orchard-land under the walls: a raiding party moving through fig trees — and a YOUNG TROJAN PRINCE, Lycaon, caught alone cutting fig-shoots for chariot-rails by lamplight, seized almost gently, too surprised to fight.
> CAPTION: One night-raid more, small and almost bloodless: in the king's own orchard the Greeks took a boy-prince cutting fig-wood for chariot rails — Lycaon, a son of Priam, seventeen and unlucky.
**PANEL 2.** The slave-market of Lemnos: Lycaon sold across the water; coins counted into a Greek hand; the boy's stunned face.
> CAPTION: Achilles did not kill him. He sold him — to Lemnos, for a fine silver bowl, as the usage of war allowed. And kin ransomed the boy, and he came home to Troy, and wept, and feasted, and swore never to leave the walls again; and eleven days he kept the oath.
**PANEL 3.** The panel the future casts backward: the boy's face, and behind it, faint as a watermark, a river in flood.
> CAPTION: On the twelfth day, princes — but no. That meeting belongs to the river, and the river belongs to the wrath, and the wrath is not yet. Only ledger this: Achilles spared Lycaon once, in the years when Achilles still spared. When they meet again, the world will have ended in between. Remember the fig orchard. Remember the silver bowl. The scene they are building is the most terrible quiet scene in all the songs of men.

### PAGE 23 — Troilus. *(restraint absolute)*
**PANEL 1.** Evening at the fountain-house outside the walls: TROILUS, youngest son of Priam — a boy exercising his horses, laughing, unguarded in the long peace of a stalemate dusk.
> CAPTION: And once — I will tell it once and briefly, as the old song does — Achilles set an ambush at the fountain-house where Priam's youngest, Troilus, watered his horses in the dusk. A boy, princes. The stalemate had made the evenings feel safe. Nothing about that war was safe.
**PANEL 2.** The chase: the boy flat on his horse's neck at full stretch for the shrine of Apollo on its knoll, Achilles's shadow long behind; the sanctuary steps ahead.
> CAPTION: He ran for the god's own precinct, as the age's law entitled him — sanctuary is sanctuary, for a boy above all.
**PANEL 3.** AFTERMATH ONLY: the shrine steps at dark; a riderless horse standing with hanging head; within the precinct door, torchlight and the shadows of Trojans lifting something with terrible care; and above the lintel, the god's emblem catching the light — watching.
> CAPTION: It did not save him. Achilles killed the boy inside Apollo's own precinct — the deed the songs themselves handle with tongs, and so do I. Troy mourned its youngest behind closed gates. But lift your eyes to the lintel, princes, and ledger what the god ledgered: blood of a suppliant boy, on Apollo's own stones, by Achilles's own hand. The Far-Shooter forgets nothing and forgives less. There is an arrow, years from now, at a certain gate, that flies too straight for any mortal archer. It was fletched on this page. When it flies, remember Troilus.

### PAGE 24 — The crime.
**PANEL 1.** The bright sea off the camp, a fishing skiff: [PALAMEDES] hauling a net, at ease, off guard — and in the boat with him, [ODYSSEUS] and DIOMEDES, faces pleasant.
> CAPTION: And now the page I would omit if my trade allowed omission. In a raid-quiet season, Odysseus and Diomedes took Palamedes fishing — Palamedes the clever, who had turned the plow at Ithaca, and who all these years had gone on being publicly, correctably clever at Odysseus's expense.
**PANEL 2.** OFF-PANEL DEED — the composition refuses it: only the empty bright water, the skiff seen far and small from the beach, three figures becoming two; gulls.
> CAPTION: He did not come back from the fishing. The old song says it in four words and moves on, and I will match it: they drowned him there. No trial, no quarrel recorded, no god's command claimed. The cleverest man in the army, murdered by the second-cleverest, for the crime of proving it.
**PANEL 3.** The beach at evening: the skiff drawn up; [ODYSSEUS] walking away up the sand alone, face absolutely ordinary; and far down the shore, the grave-mound being raised with full honors for a "drowning."
> CAPTION: They raised him a fine mound and mourned him handsomely. Princes, I have shown you Odysseus saving the army's rites, untying the wedding-knot, measuring Troy for its fall — and I show you this with the same voice, because the man is one man and the songs do not sort him for your comfort. Ledger it whole: in Nauplia an old father, Nauplius, will wait for a son who never returns, learn the truth, and nurse a patience more terrible than any battlefield in this cycle. When the war ends and the fleets sail home past a certain cape at night, watching for harbor-fires — remember who lit them, and why. The sea keeps every account we throw into it. Every one.

### PAGE 25 — The sight of Helen.
**PANEL 1.** A truce-quiet dusk; and on the walls above the Scaean gate, arranged as if by chance that is not chance: [HELEN] at the parapet — while below on the plain, drawn there he knows not why, [ACHILLES] looks up; between and about them both, faint gold in the air, the shimmer of two goddesses' work.
> CAPTION: One strange evening the old song insists on, and scholars of the songs have wondered at it ever since: Achilles desired once to look on Helen — on the cause, the face under all the arithmetic — and Aphrodite and Thetis themselves, the song says, arranged the seeing.
**PANEL 2.** The two regarding each other across the impossible distance: the world's beauty and the world's doom, each studying what the other's existence has cost.
> CAPTION: What passed in that long look, no singer has ever claimed to know, and I am not fool enough to be the first. Only this: the two most fated beings in the whole war looked at one another once, in a goddess-made quiet — the prize of the war and the price of it — and then the evening ended, and neither was changed, and everything went on exactly as fated. Perhaps that was the point, princes. Perhaps even they needed, once, to see that the other was real.
**PANEL 3.** [ACHILLES] walking back to the camp in the dark, [PATROCLUS] falling in beside him wordlessly; the walls behind, one lamp burning at the parapet.
> CAPTION: He never spoke of it, except once, obliquely, to Patroclus. The lamp on the wall burned till dawn. Make of it what you will; the song did, and kept it.

### PAGE 26 — The army breaks — and is held.
**PANEL 1 (wide).** The ninth-year mutiny: a surge of soldiery abandoning the siege-lines for the ships — gear flung down, hulls shoved at, officers swept aside; the whole war dissolving in an afternoon.
> CAPTION: In the ninth year the army's heart broke, all at once, as armies' hearts do. Not from a defeat — from the arithmetic of the years. They ran for the ships in their thousands, and the war came within one afternoon of simply... ending.
**PANEL 2.** And in the path of the rout: [ACHILLES] — alone, unarmed, planted — the one man the whole army cannot shove past; his voice like weather; the flood piling up against his stillness.
> CAPTION: And the man who stopped them — hear it, princes, and file it against everything that comes — was ACHILLES. The old song gives him this, before the great song gives him the opposite: he stood in the road of the rout, one man against the army's whole despair, and shamed and blazed and ORDERED them back to the lines, and was obeyed. He held the war together with his two hands.
**PANEL 3.** The lines re-forming at dusk, sullen but re-formed; [ODYSSEUS] watching Achilles from a distance, expression unreadable; the stone-serpent memory keyed by a nine-scratch tally on a hull.
> CAPTION: Remember this page doubly. Once for the irony the songs love: in the tenth year it is Odysseus who must stop the identical rout, with the serpent-omen I watched him bank at Aulis — and the reason Achilles is not there to do it will be the greatest song of all. And once for the plain truth under the irony: whatever the wrath will cost, the war reached its tenth year because this man refused to let it die in its ninth. The scales of Achilles, princes: begin loading them now. You will be weighing him for the rest of your lives. I still am.

---

### PAGE 27 — Troy endures.
**PANEL 1.** Inside the walls, the same years compressed: [HECTOR] everywhere at once — walls, granaries, drill-yard, gate-watch; thinner than nine years ago; unbroken.
> CAPTION: And Troy? Troy endured, because Hector spent himself like coin. Nine years of walls held and sorties led and allies managed and a brother's war carried on shoulders that never once put it down in public. The city ate because he counted; it hoped because he did not visibly despair.
**PANEL 2.** The small hours: [HECTOR] and [ANDROMACHE] and an infant — ASTYANAX — by a single lamp; the commander of Troy asleep sitting up with the baby on his chest; Andromache keeping the lamp.
> CAPTION: And in the ninth year a son was born to Hector and Andromache. His father named him Scamandrius, after the river; but the whole city called him Astyanax — "lord of the city" — because, said the people, Hector alone kept the city, and this was Hector's son. Ledger the little name with the greatest care in the whole ledger, princes. I can say no more without breaking my own heart ahead of schedule.
**PANEL 3.** Dawn from the bastion: [HECTOR] watching the enemy camp wake, ten thousand smoke-threads rising; his face, close: no hatred in it — only measurement, and endurance, and behind both, held where the city cannot see it, the knowledge.
> CAPTION: Did Hector believe Troy could win? The great singer will let you hear him answer that himself, to his wife, in words no summary may touch. Until then, watch him do the thing that is harder than believing: continuing.

---

### PAGE 28 — The allies of Troy.
**PANEL 1 (wide).** The muster of the allies within the walls — the Cypria's own closing catalogue as one procession: contingent standards streaming to the citadel: Thracian horse-lords, Paeonian archers, Carians in gold like girls for a festival, Phrygians, Maeonians, Mysians (under a young tawny commander — EURYPYLUS grown, one beat), Lycians at the head.
> CAPTION: The old song closes its account of the years the way I will close mine: with the roll of Troy's allies — for by the ninth year half of Asia stood inside those walls. Thracians, Paeonians, Phrygians, Maeonians, Carians decked in gold; and Mysia — yes — under a tawny young commander whose father once swore to guide and never fight. The son made no such oath. Watch him.
**PANEL 2.** At the column's head, received by [HECTOR] with the wrist-clasp of equals: [SARPEDON] of Lycia, grave and kingly, and keen [GLAUCUS] at his shoulder. (births refs/sarpedon.png, refs/glaucus.png)
> CAPTION: And first of all the allies, worth all the rest together: the Lycians — Sarpedon their king, a son of Zeus himself, come a thousand miles to honor an alliance he could have ignored; and Glaucus his cousin and second, of a line that will matter more than he yet knows. Sarpedon fights for Troy out of pure obligation nobly read, princes — no stolen wife, no old grudge; only the oath of allies, honored at full cost. Heaven itself will weep over what that costs him. I mean that literally. You will see the sky weep blood for this man.
**PANEL 3.** The war-council of both bloods: [HECTOR] and [SARPEDON] over the sand-table, allied kings ringed; two honorable men bearing a war neither made.
> CAPTION: So the tenth year opened with the best of Greece camped against the best of Asia, and the two most honorable men in the entire war both fighting — mark it — for Troy. The songs are not simple, princes. That is why they are true.

### PAGE 29 — The priest on the shore.
**PANEL 1 (wide, letterbox).** Dusk on the long beach in the tenth year: the camp's fires kindling — and walking up the tide-line toward them, alone, small against the ships, an OLD MAN in white: [CHRYSES], the gold staff wound with scarlet fillets in one hand, a chest of ransom borne behind him by two servants. (births refs/chryses.png)
> CAPTION: And so we come to the evening where every road in six songs has been leading. In the tenth year, up that beach at dusk, came an old priest of Apollo — Chryses — carrying a king's ransom and the fillets of the god, to beg back his daughter from the lords of the Achaeans.
**PANEL 2.** His face, close: dignity, dread, and the fillets trembling only slightly; the firelit camp ahead; the wall of ship-sterns rising over him.
> CAPTION: You know his daughter, princes — Chryseis, swept up at Thebe, allotted to the king of kings. And you know, by now, exactly how well the king of kings receives requests that touch his prizes and his pride.
**PANEL 3.** The panel holds at the threshold: the old man stopped at the firelight's edge before the assembly-ground, staff raised in supplication toward the shadowed kings; everything about to begin.
> CAPTION: Here the old songs of the gathering end, and here the greatest song that men possess draws its first breath. What the priest asked; what the king answered; what the god did about the answer; and what one quarrel in one tent then cost the world — that, princes, is the SONG OF THE WRATH itself. Tomorrow I sing you Homer.

### PAGE 30 — The frame at the threshold. [STYLE-FRAME]
**PANEL 1.** The hall, electric-quiet; the NELEID-PRINCE half-risen.
> NELEID-PRINCE: Tomorrow — the Iliad itself, father? The very song?
**PANEL 2.** The SINGER, and for the first time in six nights, something like joy.
> SINGER: The very song, child — the one all these nights were built to hold up, as a setting holds a stone. Six evenings I have sung you causes. Now you are fit to hear what they caused — fit as few hearers have ever been made fit. When the wrath begins tomorrow, you will know the weight of every name in it.
**PANEL 3.** The SINGER, rising, the phorminx held now like a sacred thing.
> SINGER: Sleep well and deeply. Tomorrow, the anger of Achilles, son of Peleus — and its devastation.

### PAGE 31 — The frame closes. [STYLE-FRAME]
**PANEL 1 (wide).** The hall emptying in murmurs of anticipation; fathers explaining to children on their shoulders.
> SINGER: Enough. Nine years are sung. The priest stands at the fires.
**PANEL 2.** The NELEID-PRINCE, last of all at the door, looking back.
> NELEID-PRINCE: Father — one thing. In all six nights... has anyone in this song been happy?
**PANEL 3.** The SINGER, alone by the embers, and his answer to the empty hall as much as to the boy.
> SINGER: Odysseus was, child — on a poor island, for a handful of years, with a quiet wife and an olive-tree bed. Hold the question. Ask it again of me in twenty nights, when the last song ends on that same island. The whole cycle, boy — the whole cycle is the distance between your question and its answer.
