### PAGE 5 — The swan.
**PANEL 1 (wide).** Dusk on the Eurotas: reeds, still water, swans among the reeds; [LEDA] alone at the bank making her offering.
> CAPTION: One evening, at the hour of offerings, the queen was alone at the river.
**PANEL 2.** High above: a great EAGLE stooping out of the darkening sky in pursuit of a single great white SWAN [ZEUS-SWAN], the swan's radiance faint against the dusk.
> CAPTION: Out of the sky came a great swan, fleeing an eagle — so it seemed.
**PANEL 3.** The radiant swan sheltering against [LEDA], her arms half-raised around it in wonder; the eagle gone; a faint golden light on her face.
> CAPTION: It took shelter in the arms of the queen. And the singer will say plainly what the wise already know: the swan was Zeus, king of the gods, in a borrowed shape. The eagle too was his device.

### PAGE 6 — The wonder.
**PANEL 1.** Months later: the palace hearth-shrine at night; a NURSE recoiling in awe, hand to mouth; on the shrine, in a basket of wool, A GREAT EGG shining faintly like a lamp behind cloth.
> CAPTION: In the fullness of time the queen was delivered — not of a child, but of a wonder: a great egg, glowing like the moon behind cloud.
**PANEL 2.** [TYNDAREUS] and [LEDA] before the shrine, the egg between them; his face troubled and reverent, hers calm.
> TYNDAREUS: This is the god's doing. What comes of it we will raise as our own — and be silent, and be glad.
**PANEL 3.** Close: the egg beginning to crack with light along the seam.
> CAPTION: And the king spoke wisely. For no house refuses what heaven sends — twice.

### PAGE 7 — Four children.
**PANEL 1.** The opened shell glowing on the shrine; TWO NURSES each holding a pair of INFANTS wrapped in wool; [LEDA] reaching for them, [TYNDAREUS] behind.
> CAPTION: From the egg came four children: two daughters and two sons.
**PANEL 2.** Years on: the four as children in the palace court — the girl HELEN [HELEN-CHILD] radiant even at play; her sister CLYTEMNESTRA dark-haired and grave beside her; the twin boys wrestling. (births refs/helen-child.png)
> CAPTION: Helen and Clytemnestra; Castor and Polydeuces. Two, the wise said, were the god's children — Helen and Polydeuces. Two were the king's — Clytemnestra and Castor. All four were raised as one blood.
**PANEL 3.** The twin boys older, racing horses on the river flat, laughing.
> CAPTION: The boys grew into the first horsemen and boxers of Greece. Men called them the Dioscuri — the youths of Zeus.
> FOOTNOTE: *DIOSCURI — "the youths of Zeus," the name all Greece gave the twin brothers.

### PAGE 8 — SPLASH: the children of the egg.
**PANEL 1 (full page).** The royal family presented on the palace steps at a festival: [TYNDAREUS] and [LEDA] enthroned; the four children before the people — [HELEN-CHILD] at center, a radiance about her that the artist gives only to her; the crowd's faces turned to her like flowers to the sun.
> CAPTION (ornate): Four children of one wondrous birth. But on one of them the light fell differently. Word of the girl Helen's beauty ran through Greece like fire through summer grass — and beauty of that measure is not a gift only. It is a burden laid on the world.

### PAGE 10 — The seizure.
**PANEL 1 (wide).** A riverside festival of Artemis*: garlanded girls dancing in a ring by the Eurotas, [HELEN-CHILD] among them; priestesses; holiday crowd.
> CAPTION: At the festival of Artemis by the river, the girls of Sparta danced.

**PANEL 3.** [TYNDAREUS] on the palace steps as the news comes, staggering; [LEDA] gripping his arm; behind them the twin brothers' faces going hard as bronze.
> CAPTION: Against the house of a king. Against the peace of a festival. Against a child. Heaven keeps a ledger of such deeds.

### PAGE 12 — The rescue.
**PANEL 1 (wide).** The Dioscuri leading a Spartan war-band over the hills into Attica at dawn, spears like a moving forest.
> CAPTION: The Dioscuri led the men of Sparta into Attica. Athens itself shut its gates and gave up the secret: Aphidna.
**PANEL 2.** Aphidna's gate broken open; [CASTOR] and [POLYDEUCES] entering; [HELEN-CHILD] running to them; no slaughter shown — the fort yields.
> CAPTION: Aphidna fell in a day. The brothers took no revenge on the land — they had come for one thing only.
**PANEL 3.** The homeward road: [HELEN-CHILD] riding before [CASTOR]; walking beside the horse, a captive now, the old woman [AETHRA].
> CAPTION: And they brought their sister home. One prize they took: Aethra, mother of Theseus, made handmaid to the child she had guarded. Mark that old woman well, princes. Her road runs further than any in this hall would guess — it ends on the walls of Troy.

**PANEL 1 (wide).** The road to Sparta crowded with the retinues of kings: chariots, gift-wagons, banners, ships' crews come up from the coast.
> CAPTION: They came from every kingdom: the young lords of all Greece, each with gifts to empty a treasury.
**PANEL 2.** Inside the great court, the suitors presented — a line of magnificent men: huge AJAX with his tower shield (births refs/ajax.png); keen young DIOMEDES (births refs/diomedes.png); grave IDOMENEUS of Crete (births refs/idomeneus.png); and PHILOCTETES bearing a great bow.

### PAGE 16 — The danger.
**PANEL 1.** The feast hall at night: suitors at table, but eyes cutting sideways at rivals; two young kings half-risen over an insult, hands near sword-hilts; others pulling them down.
> CAPTION: Forty kings under one roof, wooing one woman. The wine went round, and under the laughter the hall smelled of drawn bronze.
**PANEL 2.** [TYNDAREUS] sleepless by a lamp, an old COUNSELOR beside him.
> TYNDAREUS: If I choose the strongest, the rest unite against him. If I choose the richest, the proud ones burn my roof. Whichever man I name, I name a war.
**PANEL 3.** Close on the counselor, shaking his head; through the doorway behind, the crowded hall.
> COUNSELOR: Then find a man clever enough to make the choosing safe, my king. If such a man lives.

### PAGE 17 — Odysseus.
**PANEL 1.** The gate: a single plain traveler arriving on foot with two companions and no gift-wagons — [ODYSSEUS], compact, watchful, half-smiling. (births refs/odysseus.png)
> CAPTION: Among the last came a young king from a poor rocky island in the west: Odysseus of Ithaca. He brought no gifts. He came to watch.
**PANEL 2.** The hall: [ODYSSEUS] leaning against a pillar, arms folded, reading the room — the rivalries, the danger — like a man reading a game board.
> CAPTION: For Odysseus had the gift the gods give more rarely than strength or beauty: mētis* — the cunning mind. He saw at one glance what Tyndareus feared. And he saw something else.
> FOOTNOTE: *MĒTIS — cunning intelligence; among the Greeks an excellence of the first rank, the special gift of the goddess Athena.
**PANEL 3.** Across the court, at a well among the household women: [PENELOPE], the king's niece, drawing water — meeting Odysseus's look for one steady moment, then lowering her eyes. Chaste, brief, decisive. (births refs/penelope.png)
> CAPTION: Penelope, daughter of the king's brother Icarius. Not the most beautiful woman in that palace — but the one whose eyes were quiet and deep. Odysseus made his choice in that moment, and it was not Helen.

### PAGE 18 — The bargain.
**PANEL 1.** Night terrace: [ODYSSEUS] and [TYNDAREUS] alone under the stars.
> ODYSSEUS: You cannot choose a husband, king. But I can make the choosing safe — so safe that no sword leaves its sheath, now or after.
**PANEL 2.** [TYNDAREUS] gripping the parapet, turning.
> TYNDAREUS: Untie this knot, Ithacan, and name your price.
**PANEL 3.** [ODYSSEUS], the half-smile gone, entirely serious.
> ODYSSEUS: My price is a marriage — but not Helen's. Speak for me to your brother Icarius. I ask for Penelope.

### PAGE 19 — SPLASH: THE OATH OF TYNDAREUS.
**PANEL 1 (full page).** Torchlit night court of the palace: a sacrificed horse laid with dignity on a stone of oath, covered to the neck with a dark cloth; the SUITORS in a great ring around it, every right hand raised; [TYNDAREUS] with staff at the head; [ODYSSEUS] at the ring's edge, the author of the scene, watching it bind.
> CAPTION (ornate, top): This was Odysseus's device. Before any man was chosen, EVERY suitor swore the great oath upon the sacrifice: to defend the marriage of Helen's husband — WHOEVER he might be — against any man on earth who should ever take her from him.
> CAPTION (ornate, bottom): So every rival became a guardian, and the choosing was made safe. But hear it, princes: an oath is a seed. This one lay quiet in the ground for years — and when it broke the earth at last, it dragged every king who swore it, and their ships and their sons, across the sea to Troy.

### PAGE 20 — The choosing.
**PANEL 1.** The great court, day: [TYNDAREUS] rising with his staff; the suitors tense; [HELEN] standing before the throne with a myrtle garland in her hands.
> CAPTION: Then the king chose — and the tradition is honest about the reasons: he chose the wealth and power of Mycenae.
**PANEL 2.** [HELEN] placing the garland on [MENELAUS] — red-gold hair, honest open face — while behind him stands his elder brother [AGAMEMNON], black-bearded, in purple, satisfied. (births refs/menelaus.png, refs/agamemnon.png)
> CAPTION: Menelaus, younger son of Atreus, backed by his brother Agamemnon's gifts. The sons of the cursed house of Mycenae had risen high indeed.
**PANEL 3.** The rival suitors — Ajax, Diomedes, Idomeneus, the rest — swallowing their anger, bound; some already smiling and raising cups, the oath holding.
> CAPTION: And no sword left its sheath. The oath held them all — as it would hold them all again.

### PAGE 21 — The wedding of Helen.
**PANEL 1 (wide).** A wedding procession at evening in the manner of the old songs: torches, myrrh and cassia smoking in censers, singing girls, [HELEN] veiled in gold beside [MENELAUS] in a garlanded chariot, the crowd casting flowers.
> CAPTION: The wedding of Helen and Menelaus was the greatest festival Sparta had ever seen. The singers sang till dawn.
**PANEL 2.** [TYNDAREUS] placing Menelaus's hand on the royal staff of Sparta, [HELEN] beside them.
> CAPTION: And Tyndareus, grown old, gave more than his daughter: in time the rule of Sparta itself would pass to Helen's husband.
**PANEL 3.** [HELEN] at the top of the palace steps looking out over the torchlit valley, her face unreadable, beautiful, faintly sad.
> CAPTION: So Helen was wed, and Sparta rejoiced. Whether Helen rejoiced, the songs do not say. Remember that too.

### PAGE 22 — The price claimed.
**PANEL 1.** [TYNDAREUS] speaking with his brother [ICARIUS], gesturing toward [ODYSSEUS] who waits apart; Icarius's face torn. (births refs/icarius.png)
> CAPTION: Then Tyndareus paid Odysseus's price. He spoke to his brother Icarius — and Icarius could not refuse the man who had saved the peace of Greece. But his heart refused.
**PANEL 2.** [ODYSSEUS] and [PENELOPE] before the household altar, hands joined, a small quiet rite beside Helen's vast one.
> CAPTION: So Odysseus wed Penelope — a small wedding after the great one, and the happier of the two.
**PANEL 3.** [ICARIUS], pleading, both hands out, to the couple beside a readied chariot.
> ICARIUS: Stay in Sparta, both of you! I will build you a house by my own — daughter, do not go to that rock in the sea!

### PAGE 23 — The veil.
**PANEL 1 (wide).** The road from Sparta: the chariot moving, [ODYSSEUS] at the reins, [PENELOPE] beside him; behind, on foot, [ICARIUS] following, still calling out, arms raised.
> CAPTION: And when they set out for Ithaca, Icarius followed the chariot on foot, begging his daughter to stay.
**PANEL 2.** [ODYSSEUS] halting the horses, turning to [PENELOPE] — not commanding; asking.
> ODYSSEUS: I will not steal a wife. Choose freely, lady: your father's house — or my road.
**PANEL 3.** Close: [PENELOPE], saying nothing, drawing her veil across her face. [ICARIUS] behind, stopped in the road, understanding.
> CAPTION: Penelope said no word. She drew her veil over her face — and that was her whole answer. Her father let her go, and on that spot he raised a shrine to Aidōs* — to Modesty herself. Mark this woman, princes. Twenty years from now, all the manhood of an island will break against her quiet will.
> FOOTNOTE: *AIDŌS — reverent modesty and shame before the eyes of others; among the Greeks, a virtue with the force of law.

### PAGE 24 — Ithaca.
**PANEL 1 (wide).** [SET-ITHACA]: the rocky island rising from a wine-dark sea, the modest hall on its saddle; the chariot's ship drawn up in the harbor below.
> CAPTION: Ithaca is a poor island — rock, goats, and good harbors. Odysseus would not have traded it for Mycenae.
**PANEL 2.** The courtyard: [ODYSSEUS] with adze and chisel, building — the frame of a marriage bed built AROUND a living olive tree, its trunk forming one post; [PENELOPE] watching from the doorway, hand to her smiling mouth.
> CAPTION: With his own hands he built their marriage bed — and he built it around a living olive tree rooted in the earth, so that the bed could never be moved without killing the tree. Only two living souls would ever know that secret. Remember the olive-tree bed, princes. At the far end of this whole long song, everything will turn upon it.
**PANEL 3.** Evening: the two on the hall porch, the sea beyond; simple, content.
> CAPTION: And there, for a while, the story leaves them: a clever king, a quiet queen, a poor island, and a happiness the great palaces might have envied.

### PAGE 25 — The other wedding.
**PANEL 1 (wide).** [SET-MYCENAE]: the Lion Gate hung with wedding garlands; a procession entering — [AGAMEMNON] with [CLYTEMNESTRA] beside him in the chariot, splendor heavier and colder than Sparta's.
> CAPTION: And the elder sister? Agamemnon of Mycenae, not content with making his brother a king, took Clytemnestra for himself. The two thrones of the Peloponnese now shared one blood twice over.
**PANEL 2.** The megaron feast at Mycenae: gold everywhere; [CLYTEMNESTRA] enthroned beside [AGAMEMNON], her watchful eyes taking the measure of the hall.
> CAPTION: The feast was magnificent. But this was the hall of Atreus — the hall of the terrible supper of the last song. Old blood does not wash out of stones.
**PANEL 3.** Far away: a lean YOUNG MAN at a poor hearth in a far country, sharpening a knife by firelight — grown AEGISTHUS, the boy of the last song.
> CAPTION: And far away, in exile, the boy from the last song — Aegisthus, son of Thyestes — had grown to manhood, nursing one lesson: remember Mycenae. Remember the feast. The threads are laid, princes. The loom only waits.

### PAGE 26 — Two queens.
**PANEL 1.** Sparta: [HELEN] radiant on her throne beside [MENELAUS], the court adoring.
> CAPTION: So the two sisters of the egg were parted to the two greatest thrones of Greece.
**PANEL 2.** Mycenae: [CLYTEMNESTRA] on her colder throne beside [AGAMEMNON], composed, unreadable.
> CAPTION: Helen, queen of Sparta. Clytemnestra, queen of Mycenae.
**PANEL 3.** The sisters' farewell, remembered: the two women embracing between chariots, foreheads together, eyes closed.
> CAPTION: They loved each other, after the way of sisters. And each, in the end, will be the ruin of her own house — one by leaving it, and one by staying. But that is far ahead of us.

### PAGE 27 — The omen.
**PANEL 1.** Back at Helen's wedding feast (the singer circles back): the courtyard tables under evening sky; guests merry.

**PANEL 2.** Above the feast: an EAGLE stooping out of the dusk, snatching a WHITE DOVE from over the tables; a few faces turning up.
> CAPTION: Over the feast an eagle stooped and took a white dove, in the sight of all.

> CAPTION: The guests laughed and drank. Two men in that court did not laugh. Remember which two.

### PAGE 28 — The scales of Zeus. [STYLE-MAIN + STYLE-DIVINE]
**PANEL 1.** [SET-OLYMPUS]: [ZEUS] enthroned, [THEMIS] beside him as in the first song, a golden tally-board or loom between them.
> CAPTION: And above the clouds, the plan of Zeus moved forward — the plan made with Themis in the first song, to ease the Earth of her burden by a great war.
**PANEL 2.** Close on [THEMIS], counting off on her fingers, measured and calm.
> THEMIS: The beauty is born, and wed where you appointed. The kings of Greece are bound by their own oath — one rope through every royal hall. And Troy in the east grows rich, and its old debt to Apollo and Poseidon stands unpaid.
**PANEL 3.** [ZEUS], looking down through the cloud-floor at the small bright lands below, sovereign and unhurried.
> ZEUS: Then all the pieces stand upon the board. Let the mortals move them now — each by his own choice. That is how my plan always walks: on the feet of men's own wills.

### PAGE 29 — Troy, meanwhile.
**PANEL 1 (wide).** [SET-TROY-CITY] years on: the city rebuilt and rich, new roofs bright, ships at the shore, herds on the plain.
> CAPTION: And Troy? Troy had rebuilt, and grown richer than ever under the boy-king of the last song — Priam the purchased, now grown grey and great, father of fifty sons.
**PANEL 2.** A grey-haired KING seen from behind on a high terrace, royal Anatolian robe, the small gold pendant of his boyhood glinting at his neck, overlooking his city.
> CAPTION: He still wore the small gold pendant of his boyhood — the ransomed boy who had watched his city burn, now the richest king of the east.
**PANEL 3.** Below the terrace: the city wall — and one stretch of masonry visibly newer and smaller-blocked than the god-laid courses beside it, masons pointing it with fresh mortar.
> CAPTION: And in his wall, rebuilt by the hands of men, the fated stretch stood where it had always stood. The gods' wage was still owed. The gods were still patient.

### PAGE 30 — The frame questions. [STYLE-FRAME]
**PANEL 1.** The Miletus hall, late; the SINGER lowering the phorminx; the NELEID-PRINCE troubled.
> NELEID-PRINCE: Then the war was already certain, father — before Helen ever left her husband's hall? Before any wrong was done?
**PANEL 2.** The SINGER, gentle and grave.
> SINGER: Certain as harvest after sowing, child. The field was sown: the beauty, the oath, the unpaid debt in the east.
**PANEL 3.** The SINGER lifting one finger — the teacher's gesture.
> SINGER: And yet — mark it well — every man and woman in this song will walk to that war on their own feet, by their own choices. The gods set the board. They do not move the hands. Both things are true at once. That is the oldest riddle in the song, and I will not solve it for you. No singer can.

### PAGE 31 — The frame closes. [STYLE-FRAME]
**PANEL 1 (wide).** The hall settling for the night, fire low; listeners reluctant to leave.
> SINGER: Enough for one night. The bride is born and wed. The kings are bound. The east is rich and owing.
**PANEL 2.** The NELEID-PRINCE, one last question, half-smiling now.
> NELEID-PRINCE: And tomorrow, father? The mountain? The apple?
**PANEL 3.** The SINGER's blind face toward the dark doorway and the stars.
> SINGER: Tomorrow, if the Muse is willing: a wedding of a goddess to a mortal man, with every god among the guests — one goddess uninvited at the door — a golden apple thrown among three queens of heaven... and far away on a mountain called Ida, a herdsman who does not know he is a prince of Troy.

### PAGE 32 — The gnomē. [STYLE-FRAME, ornamented closing panel]
**PANEL 1 (full page).** The hall empty, embers, the stool and silent phorminx in lamplight, the starry Aegean night through the doorway — the closing image of the issue, mirroring the first song's close.
