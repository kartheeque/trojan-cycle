### PAGE 2 — The frame, eighth evening. [STYLE-FRAME]
**PANEL 1 (wide).** [SET-MILETUS-HALL]: the eighth evening; the SINGER seated; the hall grim and ready.
> CAPTION: On the eighth evening they came braced, for they had been promised a breaking.
**PANEL 2.** The NELEID-PRINCE.
> NELEID-PRINCE: The truce was sworn before Zeus himself, father. Lambs and wine and the hands of kings. How does such a thing break?
**PANEL 3.** The SINGER.
> SINGER: From above, child. It breaks from above. That is tonight's first terror. The second is a man — a mortal man — who wounds two gods before sundown. And the third... the third is only a husband and wife talking on a wall. You will find it the worst of the three.

### PAGE 3 — The invocation; the council above. [STYLE-FRAME → STYLE-MAIN]
**PANEL 1.** The SINGER, face lifted; the MUSE attending in the smoke.
> SINGER: Sing, goddess, how the oath was murdered in heaven before it bled on earth.
**PANEL 2.** [SET-OLYMPUS]: the gods in council over golden cups, the plain of Troy tiny and bright below through the cloud-floor; [ZEUS] needling, sidelong, deliberate; [HERA] rigid. [FRAME CUT to STYLE-MAIN]
> ZEUS: Two goddesses has Menelaus for helpers — yet there they sit, watching, well pleased... while smiling Aphrodite stands by HER man and plucks him from death. Still — the victory is fair-haired Menelaus's. Come, let us consider: shall we stir evil war again... or make friendship between the peoples? The city of Priam might stand; Menelaus might take Helen home. Peace, perhaps?
**PANEL 3.** [HERA]'s answer — and the bargain that damns three cities of her own.
> HERA: Dread son of Cronos — what a word! Would you make VAIN all my labor, the sweat I sweated, my horses worn raw gathering the host against Priam? Do it — and no god will praise you. ...Hear my measure, so you know my hate: three cities are dearest to me in all the world — Argos, Sparta, wide-wayed Mycenae. WASTE THEM, whenever they grow hateful to you; I will not stand before them nor grudge you them — only let Troy die. Send Athena down. Let the TROJANS break the oath first.
> CAPTION: Weigh what you have just heard, princes. The queen of heaven has pawned her three best-loved cities of men — populations, cradles, altars — as the price of one city's death. When gods bargain, mortals are the coin. It was ever the exchange rate.

### PAGE 4 — The falling star.
**PANEL 1 (wide).** ATHENA'S DESCENT: the goddess dropping from heaven onto the space between the two seated armies LIKE A METEOR — a burning star trailing sparks, portent-bright; both hosts surging to their feet in awe.
> CAPTION: And Athena came down like the star the son of Cronos flings as a sign to sailors or to armies — a blazing thing, shedding sparks — and leapt to the ground between the hosts. And wonder held them all.
**PANEL 2.** The murmur running both lines — the double reading, man to man, spear to spear.
> SOLDIERS (murmured, both lines): ...Evil war again, and the screaming?... — ...or friendship between the peoples, from Zeus who deals men war and peace?...
> CAPTION: Both armies read the star, and read it both ways. Hold that panel, princes: two hundred thousand men, one portent, and hope and dread in the same breath on every face. Heaven's signs are mirrors. That is why heaven likes them.
**PANEL 3.** The goddess folding herself into the Trojan ranks in the likeness of LAODOCUS, a spearman of Antenor's house — moving through the press, hunting one particular man.
> CAPTION: And the goddess put on the likeness of a man — Laodocus, Antenor's son — and went hunting through the Trojan ranks for godlike Pandarus, son of Lycaon of the country under Ida: a master archer, a vain heart, and exactly the tool the morning required.

### PAGE 5 — The temptation.
**PANEL 1.** [PANDARUS] found among his shield-bearers; the false Laodocus leaning close, the whisper with the goddess's radiance leaking faintly at its edges. (births refs/pandarus.png)
> ATHENA (as Laodocus): Son of Lycaon — wise heart — dare a thing? Loose one swift arrow at Menelaus... and win glory and gratitude from every Trojan alive, from prince Alexandros FIRST of all. Splendid gifts he would heap on you, surely — if he saw Menelaus, Atreus's warlike son, laid on the sad pyre by YOUR shaft. Come. One arrow. Vow the Wolf-born archer* a hecatomb of firstling lambs, and shoot.
> FOOTNOTE: *THE WOLF-BORN ARCHER — Apollo Lykēgenēs, as the Lycians named him: Pandarus's own country-god, whose bow-craft was his boast.
**PANEL 2.** Close: [PANDARUS]'s face as the poison takes — vanity, glory-hunger, the arithmetic of gifts; persuasion complete.
> CAPTION: So spoke Athena — and persuaded the fool. Homer's own word for him, princes: the fool. Note what the temptation cost the goddess: nothing true. No bribe was paid, no gift was ever coming, and the glory on offer was the glory of murdering a truce. She simply found the one heart on the field vain enough to hear it as opportunity. Gods do not make men rotten. They shop for the rot that is already there.
**PANEL 3.** The bow uncased: the great weapon assembled from its case — and its history in one inset: the wild IBEX on the crag, the long stalk, the shot from ambush, the sixteen-palm horns worked and joined and gold-tipped by the craftsman.
> CAPTION: And he uncased the famous bow: horn of a wild ibex he had ambushed himself below the crag — sixteen palms the horns — worked, joined, polished, tipped with gold by the horn-wright's craft. The song gives the weapon its whole biography before it lets it shoot. Learn why, princes: so that you understand this arrow was not an accident. It had been being prepared, by vanity and craft and patience, for years. Most treacheries have.

### PAGE 6 — The draw.
**PANEL 1.** The conspiracy of shields: Pandarus's companions closing their shields in a wall around him, hiding the crouched archer from Achaean eyes; the furtiveness itself an confession.
> CAPTION: And his good companions ringed him with their shields — lest the Achaeans see and rise before the shot. Mark the shields, princes. Honest arrows need no curtain.
**PANEL 2.** The rite of the crime: the string drawn to the breast, the iron head to the bow — and the vow whispered to the Wolf-born god: a hecatomb of firstling lambs, when home comes.
> CAPTION: He stripped the lid from the quiver and chose a feathered arrow never shot, freighted with black pains — and vowed his splendid hecatomb to Apollo of the wolf-country — and drew notch and string to his breast, iron to the bow, till the great horn rounded to a circle.
**PANEL 3.** THE LOOSE: the string's cry, the horn's deep note, and the shaft leaping away — the release rendered as pure sound made visible.
> CAPTION: And the bow SANG, and the string CRIED aloud, and the keen shaft leapt — furious to fly through the crowd of men. The truce, princes, is already dead in this panel. It merely has not yet landed.

### PAGE 7 — The flight, and the mother's hand.
**PANEL 1 (wide).** The arrow crossing the whole field in one long panel — over the seated ranks, over the marked dueling ground, over ten years — toward one red-gold head; both armies still unaware; the sky holding its breath.
> CAPTION: But the blessed gods did not forget you, Menelaus — the song turns and speaks TO him here, princes, as if the singer himself reached out —
**PANEL 2.** [ATHENA] flashing to Menelaus's side faster than the shaft: her hand FLICKING the arrow's course aside — the simile exact: AS A MOTHER BRUSHES A FLY FROM HER SLEEPING CHILD.
> CAPTION: — and first of all the daughter of Zeus stood before you and turned the piercing dart aside — brushed it from your skin AS A MOTHER BRUSHES A FLY FROM HER CHILD LYING SWEETLY ASLEEP — and steered it down where the belt's gold buckles met and the corslet doubled.
**PANEL 3.** THE STRIKE: through belt, through corslet-fold, through the inner guard — the head just grazing home; the panel tight on the impact, restrained, the wound's smallness and its worldsize consequence in one frame.
> CAPTION: Through the wrought belt it drove, through the corslet and the plate beneath, and grazed the man's flesh — and out came the dark blood.

### PAGE 8 — SPLASH: THE TRUCE DIES.
**PANEL 1 (full page).** The whole field in the instant after: at center [MENELAUS] standing struck, the shaft in the belt, THE DARK BLOOD running down the white thighs — rendered with the song's own simile as an inset cameo: a craftswoman's hands staining white IVORY with crimson, a cheek-piece for kings' horses; beside him [AGAMEMNON] gripping his brother's arm with a face of horror, the scepter fallen; around them both armies RISING off the earth like one breaking wave — Trojan and Achaean alike surging up, reaching for stacked arms, the sworn peace visibly dying across ten thousand faces; and far above the field, small and cold, a departing star-track of light.
> CAPTION (ornate, top): As when a Maeonian or Carian woman stains white ivory with scarlet, to be a cheek-piece for horses — a king's treasure, every horseman's desire — so, Menelaus, were your firm thighs stained with your blood.
> CAPTION (ornate, bottom): And Agamemnon shuddered to see the dark blood run, and Menelaus himself shuddered — and across the whole plain the armies came up off the ground like the sea when the squall lands. The oath of the lambs and the mixed wine, the terms sworn before Zeus and Sun and the powers below — one vain man's arrow, and it is carrion. Remember, princes, that heaven itself procured this. You will need that memory in twenty nights, when heaven begins presenting its invoices about oath-breaking. The gods punish best the crimes they commission.

### PAGE 9 — The oath will be paid.
**PANEL 1.** [AGAMEMNON] holding his wounded brother, and the speech torn out of him — grief, guilt, and iron certainty in one flood.
> AGAMEMNON: Dear brother — it was your DEATH I swore with those oaths, setting you alone before Troy!... Yet the oath is not empty, the lambs' blood not wasted: IF the Olympian does not pay it now, he pays it LATE — and men pay him back with interest, with their own heads, their wives, their children. For I know this in my heart: THE DAY WILL COME when holy Ilios perishes, and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear — Zeus himself will shake the dark aegis over them all, angered for this deceit. — But YOU, Menelaus — if you die, my grief is you: I should sail home in shame to thirsty Argos, the land yearn-worthy, and leave your bones rotting in Trojan earth under an unfinished boast...
**PANEL 2.** [MENELAUS], steadying his brother in return — the wounded man comforting the whole king.
> MENELAUS: Courage — and do not frighten the army yet. The point is not in a fatal place: the wrought belt held it, and the plate beneath. It is a graze with a great noise, brother. Send for a healer.
**PANEL 3.** [MACHAON] son of Asclepius arriving through the press with his satchel: the barbed head drawn back out through belt and plate, the blood sucked from the wound, the mild salves his father had of Chiron laid on. (births refs/machaon.png)
> CAPTION: And Machaon, son of the great healer Asclepius, drew the arrow back through belt and corslet, and sucked the wound, and laid on the gentle salves Chiron once gave his father in kindness. File the healer's face, princes — armies guard such men like water in a desert, and a day comes when an arrow finds HIM, and the whole war turns on his rescue. Meanwhile — look up from the wound. The Trojan ranks are coming on under arms.

### PAGE 10 — The king walks the lines.
**PANEL 1.** THE EPIPOLESIS: [AGAMEMNON] on foot down the re-arming Achaean front — and the montage of his praises: [IDOMENEUS] grave at his Cretans' head; the two AJAXES with their cloud of spearmen; [NESTOR] marshaling chariots before footmen, wise in the old way.
> CAPTION: Then Agamemnon walked the whole front on foot — and where he found men arming keen, he fed the fire: Idomeneus, steady as a boar; the two Ajaxes with their footmen dense as a storm-cloud a goatherd sees coming over the sea; old Nestor, arraying chariots first, footmen behind, the weak in the middle where they must fight whether they will or no. "Would my knees had your heart's youth, old man," said the king. So far, a good king. Watch what pride does with the next two.
**PANEL 2.** The sting misdelivered: Agamemnon finding [ODYSSEUS] and his ranks standing idle — because the alarm had not yet reached them — and lashing them as shirkers; and the Ithacan's answer with the dark look.
> AGAMEMNON: And YOU — master of profitable tricks, cold heart! Why stand you cowering apart, waiting for others to do the dying? First at my feasts, you two — last, it seems, to the fight!
> ODYSSEUS: (the dark look) What word has escaped the fence of your teeth, son of Atreus? WE hang back? Stir the war and watch — you will see the father of Telemachus in the FRONT-FIGHTERS of the Trojans' very champions. Your word is wind.
> CAPTION: And the king, seeing the anger, smiled and took it all back — he was quick to unsay, our king of kings, which is a virtue that would be greater if he were slower to say.
**PANEL 3.** The sting that lands on iron: Agamemnon taunting [DIOMEDES] with his father Tydeus's glory — and the young man saying NOTHING, bowing to the rebuke of a king; his companion STHENELUS (a keen scarred charioteer, no ref) retorting hotly — "WE are better than our fathers; we took seven-gated Thebes!" — and [DIOMEDES] silencing his OWN defender with a level look.
> DIOMEDES: Quiet, friend. Sit down and hear me. I hold no grudge against Agamemnon, shepherd of the people, for whetting the Achaeans to fight: his will be the glory if we take Troy — and his the great grief if we are cut down. Come — let you and me remember our OWN fury.
> CAPTION: Study this exchange like scripture, princes. The king insults him unjustly; he takes it in silence for the office's sake; he silences his own defender; and then he goes and answers with deeds all day. His father's friends called Tydeus a small man who was a great fighter. The son, you are about to learn, is a great fighter who is also a great man. The song is going to give him this entire day. It is called an ARISTEIA* — and his is the pattern of them all.
> FOOTNOTE: *ARISTEIA — a hero's day of supreme excellence, when the song pours all its light on one man's fighting.

### PAGE 11 — The armies meet.
**PANEL 1 (wide).** THE CLASH: the two hosts meeting at last — LIKE TWO WINTER TORRENTS from great mountain springs hurling their water together in one ravine, heard by the shepherd far off in the hills; shield on shield, spear on spear, the roar made visible.
> CAPTION: And as two winter rivers, flooding down the mountains from their great springs, hurl their water together where the ravines meet — and the shepherd hears the thunder far off in the hills — such was the roar and the labor as the armies came together.
**PANEL 2.** The first blood of the broken truce: young ANTILOCHUS, Nestor's son, striking down the first Trojan champion of the day; the grim ledger of names beginning; gains and losses swaying.
> CAPTION: Antilochus killed first — Nestor's quick son, first blood of the oath-breaking — and man answered man down the whole line, and the earth began its long drink. The song names them, princes, every one: who, whose son, how, and what home will miss them. I cannot carry every name tonight. But know that the song can, and does, and holds that the naming IS the honoring. No man dies anonymously in Homer. It is the poem's deepest courtesy.
**PANEL 3.** Above the struggle, half-seen in the battle-haze: [ARES] raging up and down the Trojan side, huge and brazen; [ATHENA] steadying the Achaean; Terror and Rout and Strife insatiable moving between. (births refs/ares.png)
> CAPTION: And Ares drove the Trojans, and grey-eyed Athena the Achaeans — and Terror and Rout worked the field, and Strife whose fury never fills, sister of murdering Ares: small at first when she rises, but her head strikes heaven while she walks the earth. The gods you glimpse in the smoke tonight, princes — before this day ends, two of them will bleed.

### PAGE 12 — The prayer of the wounded man.
**PANEL 1.** [PANDARUS]'s second arrow: the shaft taking [DIOMEDES] clean through the right shoulder-plate as he storms the front — the archer's exultant shout from cover, boasting to all Troy of the second triumph.
> PANDARUS: Forward, Trojans! The best of the Achaeans is hit! — I say he will not bear up long against my bolt — not if the Wolf-lord truly sent me from Lycia!
> CAPTION: The archer of the broken truce, at his trade again: Diomedes, through the shoulder, the blood spurting over the corslet — and Pandarus boasting to the whole army. He has perhaps an hour to live, princes. The song lets him spend it exactly like this.
**PANEL 2.** Behind the shield-wall: STHENELUS drawing the arrow clean through and out; the blood; and [DIOMEDES] not retreating a step — PRAYING, war-hot, the wound ignored.
> DIOMEDES: Hear me, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, unwearied one! If ever you stood by my father in the heat of battle — STAND BY ME NOW. Grant me to kill that man — bring him in my spear's cast — who shot me before I saw him and boasts over me that I shall not long see the sun!
**PANEL 3.** THE GIFT: [ATHENA] beside him, unseen by all others — the limbs made light, the old fury of Tydeus set burning in the son — and the greater gift and its one commandment.
> ATHENA: Courage, Diomedes: I have put your father's fearless heart in your chest, and TAKEN THE MIST FROM YOUR EYES that till now lay on them — so you shall know a god from a man on this field. Therefore: if a god comes to test you, fight NO immortal — EXCEPT if Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, comes into the battle. HER you may wound with the sharp bronze.
> CAPTION: Enter the strangest license in the whole song, princes: the mist lifted, the sight of gods granted — and one goddess, by name, marked lawful game. Athena has not forgotten a certain apple. Her feuds have a long, exact memory, and today she has found hands for one of them.

### PAGE 13 — The account of Pandarus closes.
**PANEL 1 (wide).** THE RAMPAGE: [DIOMEDES] falling on the Trojan line LIKE A WINTER TORRENT that bursts the dykes — the built banks cannot hold it, the vineyard walls go down under the sudden water — Trojan champions scattered before him rank by rank.
> CAPTION: And Diomedes went at the Trojans like the river in sudden flood that scatters the dykes — the banks cannot hold it, the vineyard walls cannot check it, when the rain of Zeus drives it — so he stormed the close ranks, and they could not stand though they were many.
**PANEL 2.** [AENEAS] seeking out [PANDARUS] through the reeling line — the recruitment: your bow, this menace, now; and the archer's bitter reply (two shots, two failures, the bow cursed) before mounting Aeneas's chariot, spear in hand instead.
> PANDARUS: Your menace is the son of Tydeus — I know the shield, the helmet-socket, the horses. And I tell you, lord — twice I have hit their best today, Menelaus and this one, TWICE drawn true blood — and only waked them fiercer. My bow was born under a curse. If ever I see home, may a stranger's hand cut my head off if I do not snap this thing across my knee and feed it to the fire — it follows me like wind!
> CAPTION: Hear the fool arrive at wisdom one hour late, princes: he blames the BOW. To the last breath, the instrument, never the hand. Come — the song is done with him. Aeneas takes him up into the chariot, and they charge the man with the lifted mist.
**PANEL 3.** THE CLOSE: Pandarus's great cast from the chariot punching through Diomedes's shield to lodge in the corslet — his premature shout of triumph — and [DIOMEDES]'s answering spear, Athena-guided, ending him mid-boast; the truce-breaker down in the dust, the account closed.
> CAPTION: Pandarus's last throw pierced the shield and touched the corslet — and he shouted victory with the life already leaving him to say it in. For the spear of Diomedes came back at the shout, and Athena drove it home, and the archer of the broken oath went down in the dust between the armies his one arrow had set back to killing each other. Ledger closed, princes — closed by the very man his second arrow marked. The song's arithmetic is never showy. It is merely exact.

### PAGE 14 — The stone, and the mother.
**PANEL 1.** [AENEAS] leaping down to stand over Pandarus's body — spear and shield, LIKE A LION over the kill, terrible in defense of the fallen ally.
> CAPTION: And Aeneas leapt down with shield and long spear and bestrode the body like a lion in its strength — for the Dardanian would not let even a rash ally lie unguarded. Whatever else the years show you of this man, princes, they will never once show you desertion. Remember it in the ash of Issue Eighteen.
**PANEL 2.** THE STONE: [DIOMEDES] heaving up a boundary-stone — A WEIGHT NOT TWO MEN OF TODAY'S MEN COULD LIFT — and wielding it ALONE, easily; the cast crushing Aeneas's hip-socket; the hero down on one knee, one hand in the earth, the dark coming over his eyes.
> CAPTION: Then Tydeus's son took up in one hand a stone — a great deed — a weight not two men could carry, of men such as men are NOW; and he wheeled it and crushed the cup of Aeneas's hip, and the hero went down on his knee with his fist in the earth, and black night began to cover his eyes. And there — but for a mother — the tale of Rome... but no. That is not tonight's song, nor mine to sing.
**PANEL 3.** THE MOTHER: [APHRODITE] descending through the battle-light, pouring her white arms around her son, wrapping him in a fold of her shining robe — a shield against the spears — and bearing him up out of the press.
> CAPTION: For his mother had seen. Aphrodite, who bore him to the herdsman Anchises on Ida, poured her pale arms about her dear son and wrapped him in a fold of her bright robe, proof against the flying bronze, and carried him out of the war. And the man with the lifted mist saw exactly who she was — and remembered his license.

### PAGE 15 — The wounding of Aphrodite.
**PANEL 1.** THE PURSUIT: [DIOMEDES] hunting the goddess through the crowd — knowing her for what she is: NO battle-queen, no Athena, no city-sacking Enyo — a soft immortal in the iron field; the long chase through the press.
> CAPTION: And Diomedes went after her through the thick of the fighting, knowing her for a god without strength — none of those goddesses who marshal men's wars — and when he caught her through the great press, he lunged.
**PANEL 2.** THE WOUND: the spear-point grazing the base of her palm through the ambrosial robe — and the IMMORTAL BLOOD flowing: ICHOR, golden-pale light welling where blood would be; the goddess's SHRIEK; [AENEAS] falling from her arms — and [APOLLO] catching him mid-fall in a dark blue cloud.
> CAPTION: The bronze tore the skin above the palm, through the robe the Graces themselves had woven — and out flowed the immortal blood of the goddess: ICHOR, such as runs in the blessed gods, who eat no bread and drink no dark wine, and so are bloodless, and are called deathless. She screamed — and flung down her son — and Phoebus Apollo caught him in his arms in a dark blue cloud, lest some Achaean spear find his life. Two catches in one panel, princes: the god caught the hero, and the war caught a goddess. The second had never happened in the memory of men.
**PANEL 3.** THE TAUNT: the goddess fleeing, distracted with pain, IRIS leading her out of the throng — and [DIOMEDES]'s great voice hurled after her over the battle:
> DIOMEDES: YIELD, daughter of Zeus, from war and fighting! Is it not enough that you ambush the strength of WOMEN? Haunt the war, and I promise you — you will learn to shudder at the word "battle" when you hear it from a distance!
> CAPTION: And the goddess of desire fled the field of Troy — bleeding, mocked by a mortal, her wounded wrist cradled — the goddess, princes, for whose verdict on a mountain all these men are dying. The song's irony has no bottom. Do not laugh yet. Her exit is about to be gentler than anything on the field below deserves.

### PAGE 16 — Olympus receives her.
**PANEL 1.** The borrowed chariot: [APHRODITE] finding [ARES] seated at the battle's left with spear and swift horses leaning on the mist — begging his gold-frontleted team; the ascent, [IRIS] driving.
> APHRODITE: Dear brother — save me! Give me your horses to reach Olympus — I am hurt — A MORTAL wounded me — Tydeus's son, who would fight Father Zeus himself today!
**PANEL 2.** THE COMFORT: on Olympus, her mother DIONE (a stately gentle goddess, inline) gathering her in, stroking the wounded wrist — and the old consolations: gods have suffered from men before — Ares chained in the bronze jar thirteen months; Hera and Hades each stung by Heracles's shafts — endure, child; and the wrist healed under the stroking hand.
> DIONE: Endure, my child, and bear up, though it grieves you. Many of us who keep Olympus have taken hurt from men... And this Diomedes — the fool does not know the man who fights the gods does not live long; his children do not climb his knee calling "father" when he comes home from war. Let him look to it — lest a better than you meet him some day.
> CAPTION: Mark the mother's curse inside the mother's comfort, princes — "his children will not climb his knee." The song will remember that blessing-shaped threat when Diomedes's homecoming is sung. Nothing said on Olympus is ever only said.
**PANEL 3.** THE SMILE OF ZEUS: the father of gods and men calling his daughter to him, and the gentlest rebuke in the whole poem — while [HERA] and [ATHENA] needle from across the hall.
> ATHENA (silken, to Zeus): Father — will you be angry if I guess? Cyprian went urging some Achaean woman to follow her beloved Trojans... and scratched her slim hand on a golden pin.
> ZEUS (smiling, drawing Aphrodite close): Not for you, my child, are the works of war. Attend to the lovely works of MARRIAGE — and leave all this to rushing Ares and Athena.
> CAPTION: And the father of gods and men SMILED, and called the golden one to him, and set her right with a sentence. So heaven laughs its daughter whole again — while on the plain her blood-price is still being paid in men. You have seen this joint before, princes, two songs ago, at the feast after the nod. Homer keeps pressing the two worlds together and letting YOU feel the cold. It is the engine of the whole poem. Do not let it become comfortable.

### PAGE 17 — Not of the same order.
**PANEL 1.** The field: [APOLLO] making the PHANTOM — an image of Aeneas laid in the battle's midst, armor and all — and around the eidolon Trojans and Achaeans hammering each other's oxhides; while the true Aeneas lies in the shining shrine on Pergamos, LETO and [ARTEMIS] healing and glorifying him.
> CAPTION: And Apollo made a phantom — an image of Aeneas to the life, in the very armor — and around that empty thing the two armies went on killing one another. There is no colder panel in the song, princes, and no truer one: half the wars of men are fought around eidola. — The true man, meanwhile, lay in Apollo's great shrine on the height, and Leto and Artemis of the arrows healed him and made him more splendid than before.
**PANEL 2.** THE THREE ASSAULTS: [DIOMEDES] — knowing full well the god who holds the sheltering shield — charging [APOLLO] himself once, twice, THREE times, spear against the very hands of the Far-Shooter, battle-fury past the edge of everything permitted.
> CAPTION: But Diomedes — Diomedes in his fury charged APOLLO HIMSELF: three times he went in to kill his man, and three times the god turned the bright shield; the license said Aphrodite, princes, and the fury has stopped reading licenses. Watch the fourth charge.
**PANEL 3.** THE FOURTH: the god's aspect opening — no rage, worse than rage: the cold totality — and THE VOICE that ends the day's trespass; Diomedes giving back, back, back.
> APOLLO: THINK, son of Tydeus — and GIVE WAY. Do not will yourself equal to the gods. NEVER of the same order are the race of deathless gods and the race of men who walk the earth.
> CAPTION: And Tydeus's son gave ground — not far, the song says, but back — avoiding the wrath of the Archer. Write the sentence over your lintels, princes; it is the boundary-stone of the whole heroic world, spoken by its coldest surveyor: NOT OF THE SAME ORDER, GODS AND MEN. Tonight one man has bent that law double and lived. No other man in the whole cycle will get his price. Ask Patroclus. Ask, at the gate, Achilles. But those are songs to come.

### PAGE 18 — Ares takes the field; Sarpedon takes the ledger.
**PANEL 1.** [APOLLO] rousing [ARES] from his mist — "that man would fight Father Zeus; loose yourself on him" — and the war-god pouring into the Trojan lines in the likeness of a Thracian captain, rallying, driving; [SARPEDON]'s hard word finding [HECTOR]:

### PAGE 19 — SPLASH: THE WOUNDING OF ARES.
**PANEL 1 (full page).** The peak of the impossible day: [DIOMEDES]'s chariot driven straight onto the war-god — [ATHENA] herself in the car, mortal charioteer displaced, the CAP OF HADES on her helm rendering her void-dark and unseeable to Ares; the war-god's own spear already turned uselessly aside by her hand; and the great thrust going home — Diomedes's spear, ATHENA'S TWO HANDS DRIVING IT DEEPER, into the flank of [ARES] below the war-belt; the god's head thrown back in THE BELLOW — the cry of nine, of ten thousand men in one throat, rendered as concentric shock-rings flattening the battle-haze — both armies below dropping shields to clap hands over ears, a TREMBLE seizing Trojan and Achaean alike; and the wound already smoking as the god begins to rise.

### PAGE 24 — Gold for bronze.
**PANEL 1.** THE RECOGNITION: [DIOMEDES]'s spear PLANTED point-down in the earth between them — the fighting stance dissolved — the glad voice:

> CAPTION: And his dear father laughed aloud, and his honored mother — LAUGHED, princes, both of them, on the wall of the dying city, between the prophecy and the fact — and Hector took the helmet from his head and laid it all-shining on the ground. And he kissed his dear son and swung him in his arms.
**PANEL 2.** THE PRAYER: the child held up to the sky between his father's hands — and the father's whole hope, spoken to heaven, in the terms the age keeps for its greatest blessing:
> HECTOR: Zeus and you other gods — grant that this my child become, as I am, preeminent among Trojans, as strong and brave; and rule Ilios in his strength. And let a man say of him one day, "HE IS FAR BETTER THAN HIS FATHER," as he comes home from war bearing the bloody spoils of the enemy he killed — AND LET HIS MOTHER'S HEART REJOICE.
> CAPTION: Hear the shape of the hope, princes: not "let him live" — the age did not insult its sons with safety — but "let him outshine me, and let her be there, glad." Hold the prayer against everything you know is coming, and understand at last why the ledger made you memorize a baby's name three songs ago. I will say no more. The song forbids it here.
**PANEL 3.** THE SMILE THROUGH TEARS, and the parting: the child laid in [ANDROMACHE]'s arms — and she receiving him against her fragrant breast, SMILING THROUGH HER TEARS — Homer's own impossible word; Hector's hand stroking her, the last gentleness ("no man sends me to Hades before my hour — but fate no man escapes, brave or coward, once born; go home, the loom and the distaff, the work of the day; WAR IS MEN'S CONCERN — mine most of all of those born in Ilios"); the helmet taken up again; and the two partings — she walking away TURNING BACK AGAIN AND AGAIN, the tears falling; and at the gate behind Hector, [PARIS-prince] arriving at last in his splendid war-gear, gleaming and laughing LIKE A STALLION broken free to the river, and Hector's answer softened at last — "no man who is fair could scorn your work in battle; it is the WILLING that you slack" — and the two brothers going out of the Scaean gate together into the waiting plain.
> CAPTION: And she went home turning back again and again, letting the big tears fall, and in Hector's own house the serving-women raised the death-wail FOR HIM STILL LIVING — for none of them believed he would come home again from the Achaeans' hands. And Paris overtook him at the gate, glittering like the stallion that snaps his rope and gallops ringing to the river — and Hector had not the heart to be hard with him any longer, for the man had come. And the two sons of Priam went out of the gate together, toward the noise. So ends the sixth book of the master's song, princes — on a wife who mourns the living, a child who fears the crest, a laugh, a prayer, and a door. Every war that ever was, is on that wall. That is why the wall still stands, in the singing, when every stone of it is three thousand years down.

### PAGE 30 — The frame: the third terror. [STYLE-FRAME]
**PANEL 1.** The hall: many listeners openly weeping; the NELEID-PRINCE with his fists on his knees.
> NELEID-PRINCE: You warned us, father. The husband and wife on the wall — worse than the arrow, worse than the bleeding gods. Why is it WORSE?
**PANEL 2.** The SINGER.
> SINGER: Because the gods healed, child. Ichor closes like milk curdling; Ares sat down whole beside his father by supper. But there is no Paeeon for the wall-scene — no simple that closes "let me be dead before I hear your cry." The gods' wounds tonight were spectacle. The mortals' wound was a PROMISE. And you have eighteen more nights of watching it kept.
**PANEL 3.** The SINGER, rising.
> SINGER: And she told him where the wall is weak, princes. To save him. Remember — when the time comes — that the door of Troy's death was first pointed out in love.
