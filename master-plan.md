## 1. The Vision

A complete, faithful graphic adaptation of the Trojan War epic cycle — from the plan of Zeus to the stringing of the bow in Ithaca — rendered in the Amar Chitra Katha visual language (bold ink lines, flat heroic color, dignified realism), grounded in **Late Helladic IIIB Mycenaean material culture (c. 1250–1180 BCE)**, with Troy depicted as **Wilusa, a vassal city-state of the Hittite Empire**.

The work is framed as an **itihāsa** — "thus indeed it was" — in the manner of the Mahābhārata: a received sacred history, transmitted by a bard within the story-world, governed by a divine plan, moved by a moral order that the gods themselves serve, and closing each movement with its lesson. Crucially, this framing is achieved **entirely with Greek categories**: the epic tradition already contains every itihāsa element, native and attested. Nothing Indian is imported onto the page; the Indian epic model only teaches us *which Greek elements to foreground* and how to structure them. The model is ACK's 42-issue Mahābhārata: an issue-based serial epic, each issue a self-contained episode, the whole forming one continuous saga.

## 2. The Itihāsa Frame

The Mahābhārata's architecture, realized through native Greek equivalents. Sanskrit terms below are for our design notes only; the page speaks Greek.

### 2.1 The recitation frame (the Vaiśampāyana device)
The Mahābhārata is not narrated cold; it is *recited* — Vaiśampāyana to King Janamejaya, descendant of the heroes, generations after the war. Our Greek equivalent: the entire cycle is sung by **the Singer** — a blind aoidos with a phorminx — at the court of the **Neleid princes of Miletus**, Ionian lords who claimed descent from Nestor's house of Pylos. The frame audience are thus *descendants of the heroes hearing their ancestors' deeds*, exactly as Janamejaya hears of Arjuna. And there is a deep structural rhyme: Miletus is **Millawanda** of the Hittite archives — the tale of Wilusa's fall is sung in the very city the Hittite letters name. The Singer opens each issue invoking the **Muse** (the Greek Vāk/Sarasvatī — memory as goddess), and the frame returns briefly at each issue's close.
*Fidelity status: this frame is our construction (Tier F, below), but every element is historically grounded — the aoidos institution (Demodocus, Phemius in the Odyssey itself), the Neleid claim of Miletus, the Ionian cradle of the Homeric tradition.*

### 2.2 The divine plan (daiva)
**Dios boulē** — "the plan of Zeus was being accomplished" (Iliad 1.5) — is the cycle's own confession that all of it unfolds under divine design, and *Cypria* fr. 1 gives that design its content: Earth's burden, the counsel with Themis. This is the itihāsa's daiva, and the narrator's captions treat it as the Mahābhārata treats fate: visible to the reader, mostly veiled to the actors, never cancelling their responsibility.

### 2.3 The moral order (ṛta / dharma → themis, dikē, moira)
In the itihāsa frame, the gods are not the highest court. Greek epic agrees: even Zeus weighs fates on the golden scales and may not overturn **Moira**; **Themis** (divine right order) and **Dikē** (justice) stand behind the throne of heaven as Ṛta stands behind the devas. The narrator's voice speaks from this level — above the gods' quarrels, as Vyāsa's voice does.

### 2.4 The karmic engine (hubris → atē → nemesis)
Greek epic's native causal chain: transgression breeds **atē** (the blindness the gods send upon the guilty — Agamemnon names her in Iliad 19), and atē ripens into **nemesis**. Curses (**arai**) travel down bloodlines like śāpa: Myrtilus's curse on the Pelopids ripens over the whole saga into Clytemnestra's axe. Pollution (**miasma**) works like pāpa: Ajax the Lesser's blasphemy in the sack wrecks the fleets of the innocent. The narrator tracks these chains explicitly, as the Mahābhārata tracks the fruits of the dice game.

### 2.5 The violated dharma at the war's heart (xenia)
Paris's crime is precisely a violation of **xenia** — guest-dharma, atithi-dharma — committed against his host's hearth under **Zeus Xenios**, guardian of guests. The war is therefore not a bride-raid avenged but *dharma-yuddha*: the cosmos itself moving to punish the guest who defiled the host's house. The Odyssey closes the theme in ring form: the suitors are guests who defile a host's house, and die for it. The whole 26-issue arc is bracketed by the two great xenia crimes and their punishments.

### 2.6 The yugānta (Hesiod's Age of Heroes)
Hesiod (*Works and Days* 156–173) states it plainly: Zeus ended the **race of heroes** in the wars at Thebes and Troy, and after them began the **Iron Age** — our own diminished age. The Trojan War is the Greek yugānta, as Kurukṣetra ends the Dvāpara. The Singer's frame makes this explicit: he sings from inside the Iron Age about the last generation of the heroic one, with the elegiac double vision the Mahābhārata's frame carries.

### 2.7 Heroic values as the warrior's puruṣārthas
**Kleos** (undying fame) as kīrti/yaśas — Achilles's choice between long life and everlasting glory is the kṣatriya's choice, stated as starkly as anything said to Arjuna. **Nostos** (homecoming) as the gṛhastha's counter-value — the Odyssey is the epic of the man who chooses the household world and must fight through hell to reclaim it. **Timē** (honor-portion) as māna. The tension among these three drives every major decision in the cycle.

### 2.8 The didactic voice (gnomai as subhāṣita)
Each issue closes with a **gnomē** — an authentic gnomic line from Homer or Hesiod (freshly rendered), set in an ornamented caption panel exactly as ACK closes issues with a śloka: the jars of good and evil at Zeus's threshold, the enduring heart the fates have given men, and their kin. This is the phala-vākya of each episode.

### 2.9 The parallel substrate (never on the page)
Itihāsa-purāṇa parallels are used as an **interpretive substrate**: they guide characterization, scene selection, emphasis, and the narrator's moral framing — but are never named, quoted, or visually signaled. Three rules: (1) a parallel may only *amplify what is already in the Greek text*, never add what isn't; (2) parallels are heuristics, not equations — no character "is" their Indian counterpart; (3) not every episode needs one, and none is forced.

Working table of load-bearing parallels:

- **Odysseus ← Arjuna** (the Allen parallel). The year in disguise among his own (beggar in Ithaca ~ Bṛhannalā in Virāṭa's court); the humiliations endured in disguise; the bow only he can string, contested for the wife; the sequence of women encountered on the long detour (Circe, Calypso, Nausicaa, Penelope); the guiding deity at his shoulder. *Use:* stage the Ithaca books (Issues 24–26) with Virāṭa-parvan pacing — the slow burn of insult, recognition by scar, revelation at the bow.
- **Helen ← Ahalyā.** Seduced by a resplendent intruder under divine machinery; the tradition itself split on her culpability (as Rāmāyaṇa recensions split on Ahalyā's knowledge, Greek tradition splits between the willing Helen, the compelled Helen of Iliad 3 who resists Aphrodite and is threatened into submission, and Stesichorus's phantom-Helen); the restoration to the husband without destruction — Menelaus's raised sword falling from his hand at the sack. *Use:* this fixes our **Helen policy** — neither whitewash nor condemnation; a woman under daiva, whose own voice (her self-reproach in Iliad 3 and 6, her lament for Hector in 24) is given full weight. The Iliad 3 scene where she defies Aphrodite is mandatory and gets a full page.
- **Helen ← Draupadī** (already in §2.2's orbit): born of the divine plan as the appointed instrument of the culling; the woman as the war's axis who is not its cause — the cause is the transgression against her marriage.
- **Penelope ← Damayantī.** The false second svayaṃvara Damayantī announces, knowing only Nala can arrive in time, is structurally the bow contest: a remarriage rite designed so that only the true husband can win it. *Use:* frame Penelope's setting of the contest (Od. 19–21) as her own cunning mirroring her husband's — the text's hints that she suspects the beggar get quiet visual support.
- **Penelope's dilemma ← the proṣita-bhartṛkā question.** Dharmaśāstra debates precisely her case: how long does a wife wait for a husband gone abroad before the marriage lapses? Heroic-age Ithaca faces the same institutional question — the suitors' pressure is legally colorable in their world, Telemachus genuinely cannot expel them or force his mother, and sending her back to Icarius carries dowry consequences (all of this is in Odyssey 2!). *Use:* play the suitor crisis as a real constitutional deadlock, not cartoon villainy — which makes the shroud-trick a dharmic delaying strategy and the final slaughter a judgment, not a brawl. More broadly: **smṛti categories often illuminate heroic-Greek institutions (marriage, guest-right, inheritance, kingship) better than modern categories do**, both being archaic Indo-European aristocratic orders. This is a standing method, not a one-off.
- **Achilles ← Bhīṣma.** Son of a divine water-mother (Thetis ~ Gaṅgā) who tried and failed to make him deathless; the supreme warrior who knows the manner of his own death and walks toward it; the immortal mother's grief for a mortal son. *Use:* Thetis's scenes (Il. 1, 18, 24) carry the Gaṅgā-register of divine maternal sorrow — Homer already wrote them that way.
- **The Horse ← the upāyas of Kurukṣetra.** Victory by sanctioned deceit, and the tradition's own unease about it: as the Mahābhārata makes the Pāṇḍavas pay for the broken rules (the night massacre, the hollow triumph, Yudhiṣṭhira's grief), the cycle makes the Achaeans pay in the Nostoi — storms, scattered fleets, murdered kings. *Use:* the narrator explicitly links Issues 17–20 as the fruit of Issue 18's transgressions; Part IV is our Strī-parvan and Sauptika shadow.
- **Orestes ← the Renukā episode** (lightly). The son commanded into an act against his mother by a higher duty, and his eventual absolution. *Use:* tonal guidance only for Issue 20's ending — vengeance as tragedy discharged, not celebrated.

### 2.10 What we do not do
No Sanskrit on the page; no imported Hindu iconography; no forced one-to-one character mappings (Helen is *illuminated* by Draupadī — a woman born of the divine plan as the war's appointed instrument — but she is drawn and written wholly as Helen). The itihāsa frame is a lens ground in India and pointed at Greece; everything it reveals must already be Greek.

## 2A. Source Fidelity Policy

The cycle survives unevenly. Every issue declares which tier it draws from.

**Tier A — Canonical Homer (verbatim fidelity).**
The Iliad and Odyssey. Plot, scene order, speeches, epithets, and similes adapted directly from the Greek text. Dialogue is freshly translated/adapted — no reliance on copyrighted modern translations. Nothing invented that contradicts the text.

**Tier B — Attested Cycle (plot-faithful reconstruction).**
The lost cyclic poems — *Cypria*, *Aethiopis*, *Little Iliad*, *Iliou Persis*, *Nostoi* — known through Proclus's *Chrestomathy* summaries and surviving fragments. Plot skeleton is fixed by Proclus; dialogue and scene detail are reconstructed in cycle-consistent style.

**Tier C — Later Fill (used only where A and B are silent, always flagged).**
Apollodorus's *Epitome*, Quintus of Smyrna, Attic tragedy (for e.g. Iphigenia's interiority, Philoctetes on Lemnos). Never overrides A or B. Post-Homeric romanticism (e.g. Achilles–Polyxena romance beyond the cycle's own version) excluded.

**Tier F — The Frame (our construction, always visually distinct).**
The Singer at Miletus, the recitation device, issue-opening invocations and closing gnomai panels. Rendered in a distinct palette (sepia/twilight tones against the saga's full color) so the reader always knows frame from tale.

**Tier H — Historical Frame (Hittite documents). [RULING v0.5.3 — scope formally bounded]**
The epic material is the SOLE narrative spine. The Hittite corpus (the **Alaksandu Treaty CTH 76**, the **Tawagalawa**, **Manapa-Tarhunta** and **Milawata letters**, Piyamaradu, Ahhiyawa) is never narrative canon: the moment the archives narrate events beside the Singer, a second authority stands next to the Muse and the itihāsa frame dies — and the underlying identifications (Alaksandu/Alexandros, Ahhiyawa/Achaeans) are scholarly quicksand unfit to bear plot. Tier H is therefore restricted to exactly four uses:
1. **Material authenticity** — architecture, dress, seals, political geography of the Anatolian west (unrestricted; objects need no identifications).
2. **Constructive grammar** — the operating system for scenes no Greek source wrote (Wilusa council procedure, vassal obligation, treaty diction, what Hattusa's displeasure means), used the way dharmaśāstra would stage a court scene: as social physics, never as rival plot.
3. **The tablet prologue** — a single one-page documentary register opening Issue 3 ONLY: rendered as incised clay in a distinct palette (its own style block, parallel to STYLE-FRAME), speaking the archive's voice once — "the records of the Hatti name a city, Wilusa, and sea-raiders they called Ahhiyawa" — then handing off to the Singer forever. The archive never speaks again on any page of the cycle.
4. **Back matter** — Millawanda/Miletus, the Alaksandu near-homophony, and all such identifications live in source notes as the scholarly wink, never as on-page equations.
Tier H may never contradict, date, corroborate, or annotate anything the Singer sings.

**Anachronism policy.**
Homer's own anachronisms are canon and stay: iron as treasure-metal, cremation funerals, the duel culture, Phoenician traders. Everything *not* forced by the text falls back to LH IIIB: no Corinthian helmets, no hoplite panoply, no triremes, no marble temples, no coinage, no writing except Linear B (palace scenes) and Luwian hieroglyphs (Wilusa). The one famous exception Homer hands us as a gift: the **boar's-tusk helmet** of Iliad 10 — a genuine Bronze Age heirloom, and it becomes Odysseus's visual signature throughout.

## 2B. The Ethical Lexicon & Anti-Moralizing Policy

The epic runs on its own ethical operating system. Our narrator speaks from *inside* that system, exactly as ACK's narrator speaks from inside dharma — never apologizing for it, never translating it into a later morality. **Core Greek terms appear untranslated on the page**, italicized, glossed in a footnote at first use and in a back-matter glossary per issue (ACK's own convention for Sanskrit terms).

### The lexicon (the ideas that actually animate the stories)
- ***xenia*** — the guest-bond under Zeus Xenios: hereditary, sacred, stronger than politics. The concept that *explains* scenes moderns find baffling — above all Diomedes and Glaucus halting mid-battle to exchange armor because their grandfathers were guest-friends (Il. 6). Paris's crime, and the suitors' crime, are *xenia* crimes.
- ***kleos*** — undying fame, the warrior's immortality, since the afterlife offers none. (Design note, substrate only: *kleos aphthiton* is the exact cognate of Vedic *śravas akṣitam* — the same inherited Indo-European poetic formula. We are adapting a sister tradition, not a foreign one.)
- ***timē*** and ***geras*** — honor as material portion, and the prize that embodies it. **The quarrel of Iliad 1 is about *timē*, not romance**: Briseis is Achilles's *geras*, and seizing her publicly annuls his honor. Rendered this way, Achilles's rage is intelligible and enormous; rendered as jealousy, it is trivial. Same for Chryseis: her father prevails through Apollo's *timē*, not through pathos alone.
- ***mētis*** — cunning intelligence as an excellence, Athena's own domain, Odysseus's crown. Never framed as dishonesty needing excuse.
- ***aretē*** — excellence in function, not "virtue" in the moralized sense.
- ***aidōs*** — the shame-reverence before the eyes of one's community that restrains and compels; what drives Hector out of the gate to face Achilles though he knows.
- ***hiketeia*** — supplication under Zeus Hikesios: the ritual of clasping knees and chin that binds the stronger party. **The climax of the entire Iliad is a *hiketeia*** — Priam kissing the hands that killed his sons. Odysseus to Arete's knees, the suppliant Lycaon refused: the institution recurs at every hinge.
- ***moira / aisa*** — the portion; ***themis*** and ***dikē*** — right order and justice; ***hubris → atē → nemesis*** — the transgression chain (§2.4). *Hubris* is outrage against another's honor or the gods' — **never "pride" in the Christian sense**.
- ***miasma*** — pollution as objective fact, discharged by rite, not by repentance.
- ***charis*** — the reciprocal grace of favor and counter-favor binding men and gods (the logic of sacrifice: *do ut des* — as in yajña).
- ***thumos*** — the spirited self that heroes address within their breasts; ***menis*** — the cosmic-scale wrath reserved for gods and Achilles, the Iliad's first word.
- ***nostos*** — homecoming as a value rivaling *kleos*; ***philotēs*** — the bond of one's own.
- ***agōn*** — the contest as sacred institution (the funeral games of Il. 23 are ritual, not recreation).

### The Register Rule
Narration and ordinary dialogue speak in PLAIN REGISTER: short declarative sentences, no inversions, no compressed metaphor, epithets replaced or immediately explained. Even marked forms — invocations, curses, the closing gnomē — are written as complete plain-English sentences; they may carry weight and rhythm, but never inversion, fragment, or obscurity. A caption that must be read twice is a failed caption. And restraint must never delete causality: when the art declines to show a terrible deed, the narrator states it plainly — once, in dignified words, never dwelt upon — so the chain of cause and consequence stays whole. (The ACK method: Shishupala's end is narrated, not hidden.)

### The Dialogue Transparency Rule
No spoken line may depend on mythological knowledge the comic has not yet given the reader. Elevated diction is welcome; unshared allusion is not. The machinery: (1) the NARRATOR carries erudition — context lives in caption boxes; (2) first occurrences of essential proper names or divine concepts get an ACK-style asterisk footnote strip at the panel's base (one gloss, first use, never repeated); (3) speeches referencing myths outside the saga are rewritten to be self-contained (e.g., Themis states the flood rather than alluding to it); (4) deliberate withholding is permitted ONLY when the payoff is already scripted within the saga ("one was done at a table…"). Sanskrit-reader test: a reader who knows no Greek myth whatsoever must be able to follow every balloon.

### Anti-moralizing rules
1. **The gods are real.** Full polytheistic seriousness — never "myths," metaphors, or psychological projections. Sacrifice, libation, hecatomb, and omen-taking depicted with ritual accuracy and reverence, as ACK depicts yajña.
2. **Heroes weep.** Achilles weeps by the sea, Odysseus weeps at Demodocus's song, warriors weep openly and are not diminished. The stiff upper lip is Victorian; Homeric men grieve like monsoon rain.
3. **Heroes boast.** The victory vaunt (*euchos*) is proper form, not bad sportsmanship.
4. **War-prize concubinage, captivity, and slavery are the fabric of that world.** Depicted as the text depicts them — with occasional devastating pathos (Andromache's foretold fate, Briseis's lament over Patroclus) but no editorial finger-wagging and no sanitizing.
5. **No chivalric mercy expectations.** Ransom is an institution; Achilles refusing Lycaon after Patroclus's death is the wrath's terrible logic, shown unflinching. Homer's own pathos — the narrator's tenderness for dying young men, the similes that humanize both sides — is our only editorial voice, and it is enough.
6. **No romantic-love anachronism.** Helen–Paris is Aphrodite's compulsion, not a love story. Hector–Andromache is the epic's true marital ideal (*philotēs*). Achilles–Patroclus is rendered exactly as Homer renders it — the fiercest *philos*-bond in the poem, with neither later-era addition nor squeamish dilution.
7. **The afterlife is Homer's.** The strengthless dead, the shade of Achilles preferring the meanest serfdom on earth to kingship over the dead — no heaven, no hell, no consolation the text doesn't offer. This is *why kleos matters*, and the whole value system stands on it.
8. **Justice is archaic.** Blood-price (*poinē*), oath, ordeal, and the elders' judgment on the Shield of Achilles — wergild logic, not courtroom morality.
9. **Double motivation stands.** When a god prompts and a man acts, both are true — Homer's own theology of action. The narrator never resolves it into either psychology or puppetry. (The Greek face of what our substrate knows as daiva and puruṣakāra.)

## 2C. Construction Rules & the Gap Audit

### What is genuinely missing
A full audit of the 26 issues against the sources. Surprisingly little is wholly absent:

1. **All dialogue for the lost cycle poems** (Parts I, III, IV). Proclus fixes the plot; nearly every word must be composed. This is the largest single construction task — but it is *dialogue within attested scenes*, the least risky kind.
2. **The Wilusa interior** (Issue 3 and Trojan-side texture throughout). No Greek source narrates Trojan/Hittite court politics; the Hittite documents give names, treaties, and situations but no scenes. Council scenes at Wilusa and its relation to Hattusa must be constructed — modeled on attested Hittite diplomatic practice (treaty language, vassal obligations) crossed with Homer's own Trojan council scenes (Il. 3, 7 — where Antenor's peace faction urging Helen's return is *attested*, a ready-made political axis).
3. **The Singer's frame** (Tier F). Wholly ours; already codified.
4. **Daily-life connective tissue**: the war-camp economy of Issue 6 (raiding, foraging, prize-division — licensed by Achilles's own "twelve cities by sea, eleven by land"), household and workshop scenes for pacing. Sources for texture exist in abundance: the Shield of Achilles genre scenes, the Odyssey's rustic books, Homeric similes, and — for Mycenaean administration — the **Linear B tablets themselves** (Pylos ration lists, personnel rosters, and the o-ka coastal-watch tablets from the palace's final days: authentic set dressing of extraordinary poignancy).
5. **Female interiority attested only in tragedy**: Clytemnestra's ten-year resentment, Iphigenia at the altar from her own eyes (Tier C: Aeschylus, Euripides — used as licensed fill, flagged).

**Additions to the source list:** Hesiod's *Catalogue of Women* joins Tier B (it preserves the suitors of Helen and the Oath of Tyndareus at length — Issue 2 is better attested than first assumed); Sappho fr. 44 (the wedding procession of Hector and Andromache) becomes the model for all wedding scenes; the Linear B corpus joins Tier H.

**Canon decisions required (choices between attested versions, not gaps):**
- *Iphigenia:* the Cypria's own version — the deer substituted, the girl carried to the Tauri — is our Tier B canon (the tragic death-version noted in back matter). Clytemnestra's grievance stands either way: Agamemnon consented to the knife.
- *Palamedes:* the Cypria's version (drowned by Odysseus and Diomedes) over the later forged-letter stoning. Darker for Odysseus — and the cycle itself answers it (see ledger below).
- *Aeneas:* departs for Ida after the Laocoön portent, per the Iliou Persis — before the sack, not during it.

### Construction rules (binding on all invented material)
1. **Greek ethic only.** Every constructed scene must run on the lexicon of §2B — *xenia*, *timē*, *charis*, *aidōs*, *hiketeia* — and be consistent with the storyline's arc. If a scene would work equally well in a modern drama, it is wrongly built.
2. **Every violation is answered.** Any transgression we depict — attested or constructed — must be repaid by later events, visibly enough that the attentive reader can trace the chain. No free crimes; no unexplained punishments. Constructed material may never introduce a transgression the saga does not answer.
3. **Normal behavior of that society.** Invented daily life must be the ordinary conduct of Late Bronze Age heroic society as text and archaeology attest it — its hospitality, its raiding, its piety, its slavery, its feasting — never modern behavior in costume.

### The Consequence Ledger (a standing bible document)
Rule 2 formalized: a running table of every transgression and its narrative repayment. The cycle's own ledger, already balanced by the tradition:
- Laomedon cheats the gods → first sack of Troy; the gods' standing grievance dooms the city.
- Pelops betrays Myrtilus → the curse ripens through Atreus and Thyestes to Clytemnestra's axe (Issue 20).
- Paris violates *xenia* → Wilusa burns.
- Pandarus breaks the sworn truce → Troy forfeits the oath-gods' protection; Pandarus dies by Diomedes's spear.
- Agamemnon takes Achilles's *geras* → the ships burn, Patroclus dies, and his authority is broken before the host.
- Achilles desecrates Hector's body → dies at the same Scaean Gate; the gods themselves force the ransom first.
- Odysseus and Diomedes drown Palamedes → Nauplius's false beacons wreck the returning fleet (Issue 19) — the cycle's own repayment.
- The Achaeans sack shrines; Ajax the Lesser drags Cassandra from Athena's image → Athena and Poseidon scatter the victors; the *nostoi* become disasters.
- The suitors devour a host's house → the bow (Issue 26).
Every constructed transgression gets a ledger entry before its scene is scripted; if no repayment exists, the scene is rewritten.

## 3. Volume Architecture — 26 Issues

Twenty-six issues. Target ~32–40 pages per issue, ACK format. Total ≈ 850 pages.

### PART I — THE ROOTS OF THE WAR (Cypria + pre-cycle tradition + Hittite frame)
1. **The Burden of the Earth** (Tier F + B/C) — *Opens the outer frame:* the Singer at the Neleid court of Miletus, the invocation of the Muse, the Iron-Age audience of heroes' descendants. Then the tale begins — *Cypria* fr. 1: Earth groans under the multitude of men; Zeus and Themis devise the great war to lighten her — the cosmic root cause. The cursed houses that will fight it: Tantalus; Pelops and the treachery of Myrtilus; Atreus and Thyestes. And Troy's own old debt: Laomedon cheats Apollo and Poseidon of their wages, Heracles sacks Troy the first time, Hesione is given to Telamon (making Teucer half-Trojan in the Achaean camp), and the boy-prince is ransomed — Priamos, "the purchased one."
2. **The Swan and the Egg** (Tier B/C) — Zeus and Leda; the eggs; Helen, Clytemnestra, Castor and Polydeuces (with the *Cypria*'s own Nemesis-variant of Helen's birth acknowledged in a source note). Theseus abducts the girl Helen; the Dioscuri ride to Aphidna and bring her home. The suitors of Helen gather; the Oath of Tyndareus — Odysseus's device, and his price for it: Penelope. The two marriages: Helen to Menelaus, Clytemnestra to Agamemnon.
3. **Wilusa Between Empires** (Tier B/C, Tier H per ruling) — Opens with the one-page TABLET PROLOGUE (the archive's single appearance), then the Singer resumes. Narrative spine is wholly epic: Hecuba dreams she bears a firebrand; the seers' judgment; the infant Alexandros exposed on Ida, raised a herdsman. Wilusa's court and its place in the Hittite west rendered through constructed council scenes using Tier H grammar (vassal obligation, treaty diction) — texture, not plot. The wedding of Peleus and Thetis; Eris and the apple.
4. **The Judgment of Paris** (Tier B) — The judgment on Ida; Paris recognized and restored to Priam's house over Cassandra's warning; the voyage to Sparta; the abduction. Then the Dioscuri's last raid: Idas and Lynceus, the death of Castor, Polydeuces's shared immortality — the *Cypria*'s own explanation of why Helen's brothers never come for her (the question she asks herself on the walls in Iliad 3).
5. **The Mustering at Aulis** (Tier B, C flagged) — [Re-scoped v0.5.4 to restore the Cypria's two-muster structure.] The oath called in; Odysseus's feigned madness exposed by Palamedes (the grudge ledgered); Nestor; the FIRST muster at Aulis; the serpent-and-nine-sparrows omen (Calchas: nine years, the tenth takes Troy — payoff Il. 2, Issue 7); the guideless sailing; the FALSE WAR in Teuthrania — Telephus son of Heracles wounded by Achilles's Pelian spear (Achilles's debut); the storm-scattering; Achilles at Scyros and the marriage to Deidamia (Neoptolemus ledgered — the disguise-among-girls version is Tier C, noted only); Telephus the suppliant, 'the wounder shall heal,' the spear-rust healing, Telephus bound as guide (his son Eurypylus ledgered → Issue 16); the years of waiting; the SECOND summons and the armada regathered at Aulis. Ends on the eve of the true sailing.

## 4. Visual Bible — Core Commitments

### The ACK grammar
- Bold, uniform ink outlines; flat color fills; no painterly gradients except sky/sea washes.
- Heroic anatomy, dignified faces, expressive but restrained emotion.
- Violence stylized ACK-fashion: impact lines, off-panel resolution for the worst moments (Astyanax, the sack), blood minimal and symbolic.
- Omniscient narrator caption boxes carrying the epic voice; Homeric similes rendered as inset simile-panels (a signature device for this project: the lion simile drawn *as a lion panel*).
- Gods depicted ACK-deva style: greater stature, radiance halo, floating slightly when manifest; mortal-disguise forms drawn human until the reveal panel.
- **Divine-architecture license (ruling, v0.5.1):** the halls of the gods (Olympus, Poseidon's deep, Hades) may use idealized pillared architecture outside human chronology — as ACK's deva-lokas do. The Bronze Age-only rule binds every EARTHLY scene absolutely; heaven is exempt by design, which visually separates the divine world from the mudbrick world of men.

### Achaean material culture (LH IIIB)
- Boar's-tusk helmets (elite), bronze conical and zoned helmets; figure-eight and tower shields for old-generation heroes (Ajax's tower shield is textual!), smaller round shields for the young.
- Dendra-style banded cuirass for heavy champions; linen/leather corslets otherwise; greaves ("well-greaved Achaeans" — attested in bronze at Dendra).
- Naue II cut-and-thrust swords, long thrusting spears, chariots as battle taxis.
- Oared galleys/pentekonters, single mast, bird-head stem-posts (LH IIIC pictorial style), beached not anchored.
- Megaron palaces: central hearth, four columns, painted plaster, griffin frescoes; Linear B tablets in Pylos/Mycenae scenes.
- Dress: male short kilt/tunic; female flounced skirts and Minoan-Mycenaean bodices (rendered with ACK modesty conventions, as ACK handled apsaras); hair in long locks.

### Wilusa / Trojan material culture
- Troy VI/VIIa architecture: sloping cyclopean limestone walls, the great northeast bastion, mudbrick superstructure, Anatolian gate-shrines; Luwian hieroglyphic seals.
- Trojan elite dress with Anatolian/Hittite inflection: long robes, upturned-toe shoes for court scenes, Hittite-style horned crown reserved for cult images.
- The city's god: Apaliunas (proto-Apollo) — his shrine imagery Anatolian, dovetailing with Apollo as Troy's divine defender in Homer.
- Allies visually distinct: Lycians (Sarpedon, Glaucus), Thracians, Paeonians — the cycle as a map of the Late Bronze Age world.

### Ritual authenticity (shown whole, where the text has it)
Greek religion is *practiced* on the page, uncensored and unforced — depicted exactly where the sources place it, with the seriousness ACK gives yajña.
- **Sacrifice done properly:** the full Homeric sequence — barley grains, the hair from the victim's brow into the fire, the stunning blow and the *ololugē* cry, thigh-bones wrapped in double fat burned for the god, the *splanchna* tasted, the feast shared. The blood is shown; the tone is sanctity, not gore. Set-pieces the text hands us: the hecatomb to Apollo at Chrysa (Il. 1), Nestor's great heifer sacrifice with gilded horns at Pylos (Od. 3 — the fullest ritual scene in Homer), Eumaeus's pious swineherd's offering (Od. 14).
- **Oath-rite:** the lambs of Iliad 3 — hair distributed, wine poured, the curse spoken ("as this wine spills, so their brains"), throats cut; oath as sacrament, which is why Pandarus's arrow in Il. 4 is cosmic transgression, not mere foul play.
- **Battlefield rites:** *sphagia* and libation before engagement; prayer with the vow of future offerings (the *charis* contract in action).
- **Household religion:** the hearth as sacred center; libation before drinking; Zeus Herkeios's altar in the courtyard (where Priam dies — the horror of Issue 18 is precisely that it is sacrilege at the family altar).
- **Funerary rites in full:** cremation pyre, offerings, bones gathered in fat and laid in the golden urn, the tumulus, the games — Patroclus (Il. 23) and Hector (Il. 24) as the great set-pieces.
- **The nekyia as rite** (Od. 11): the dug pit, milk-and-honey, wine, water, barley, the black sheep's blood the shades must drink to speak — depicted with full procedural accuracy.
- **Mycenaean cult texture** in backgrounds where archaeology attests it: horns of consecration, rhyta, processional frescoes, figurines at household shrines; Wilusa's rites given Anatolian form (libation to Apaliunas at the gate-shrine).
- **Rule:** never inserted where the text lacks it; never trimmed where the text has it.

### Consistency system for image generation
Every recurring character gets a **locked descriptor string** (face, build, hair, signature gear, palette) used verbatim in every prompt. Locations likewise. Reference sheets generated and approved *before* any story panels. (Same workflow as the सर्वे भद्राणि पश्यन्तु card series.)

## 5. Production Pipeline (per issue)

1. **Breakdown** — books/episodes → page grid (~32–40 pp), set-pieces identified.
2. **Script** — full comic script: PAGE / PANEL / visual description / dialogue / captions. Dialogue freshly adapted from the Greek (Tier A) or reconstructed (Tier B/C), register: elevated but plain — short sentences, no archaism for its own sake.
3. **Reference lock** — any new characters/locations get descriptor strings + reference sheets.
4. **Panel prompts** — one image-generation prompt per panel, built from: style block (ACK grammar) + character descriptors + panel action + camera/composition note.
5. **Review pass** — anachronism audit against §2 policy.

## 7. Iterative Production Protocol (multi-week workflow)

The working files reset between sessions, so **a single project archive, kept by Karthik, is the canonical state**. Each session: upload the archive (or just the new pieces), work, download the updated archive.

### The archive (`trojan-cycle.zip`)
```
trojan-cycle/
  master-plan.md               ← this file
  characters.md                ← locked descriptor strings
  issues/
    issue-01/
      script.md                ← full panel-by-panel script
      prompts.md               ← one image-gen prompt per panel
      art/                     ← generated panels (textless)
        i01-pg01-pn1.png
        i01-pg01-pn2.png
        ...
  build/
    issue-01.pdf               ← assembled issue
    master.pdf                 ← the growing complete comic
```

### Naming convention
`i{issue}-pg{page}-pn{panel}.png` — e.g. `i01-pg03-pn2.png`. The script and prompts files carry the same IDs, so any panel can be regenerated or replaced at any time and the build stays deterministic.

### Division of labor
1. **Script** (Claude): full comic script per issue — page/panel/action/dialogue/captions.
2. **Prompts** (Claude): per-panel generation prompts = style block + locked character descriptors + panel action + composition note. **Panels are generated textless** — no balloons or captions baked into the art.
3. **Art** (Karthik): generate panels externally at his own pace, any number per session, named per convention.
4. **Lettering & assembly** (Claude): composite ACK-style balloons, caption boxes, and gnomē panels over the art programmatically from the script; lay out pages; rebuild `issue-XX.pdf` and re-merge `master.pdf` each session.

*Why textless art:* image generators render text unreliably; programmatic lettering gives consistent ACK typography, exact script fidelity, and lets dialogue be revised without regenerating art.

### Session ritual
- **Start:** upload `trojan-cycle.zip` (or only the new art files, if nothing else changed).
- **Work:** new script sections, new prompts, lettering of newly arrived panels, rebuild of PDFs.
- **End:** download the updated archive + current `master.pdf`. That download is the save-game.

### Change management
- Descriptor strings change only in `characters.md`, with a version note — mid-issue drift is the enemy of visual consistency.
- Any approved panel is frozen unless explicitly reopened.
- The plan file carries a version number bumped on structural change.
